[6] **C0007** During the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, it gained prominence through the monistic systems of Baruch Spinoza, who viewed nature as a single substance with both mental and physical attributes, and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who described the universe as composed of monads possessing perception and appetite. [8]

**C0009** Panpsychism experienced a resurgence in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, driven by challenges to physicalism in the philosophy of mind, particularly the "hard problem" of explaining subjective experience (qualia) from objective physical processes. [9] **C0010** Key contemporary advocates include Galen Strawson, who argues for panpsychism as the only coherent realism about consciousness; Philip Goff, who defends constitutive panpsychism where micro-experiences combine to form macro-consciousness; and neuroscientist Christof Koch, who integrates it with integrated information theory to suggest consciousness in simple systems. [3] **C0011** Prominent arguments in its favor include the continuity argument, which posits that consciousness evolves gradually rather than emerging abruptly, aligning with evolutionary biology; the anti-emergence argument, rejecting the idea that radically novel properties like mind can arise from non-mental bases; and the intrinsic nature argument, proposing that physics describes relational structures but panpsychism supplies the intrinsic, experiential essence of matter. ### Definition and Core Concept

**C0015** Panpsychism is the philosophical doctrine that consciousness, or mind-like qualities, is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of the natural world, present in all matter from the simplest particles to complex organisms. [12] **C0016** Rather than viewing consciousness as an emergent property arising solely from specific physical arrangements, such as neural activity in brains, panpsychism posits that experiential properties are inherent to reality itself. [13] **C0017** This view challenges traditional anthropocentric conceptions of mind, emphasizing that mentality is not confined to humans or animals but extends to the basic constituents of the universe. [12]

**C0018** At its core, panpsychism rests on three key tenets. **C0019** First, the ubiquity of mind holds that all physical entities possess some form of experiential quality, however rudimentary. [12] **C0020** Second, fundamentality asserts that consciousness is not reducible to non-experiential physical processes but is a primitive aspect of reality, on par with fundamental physical properties like mass or charge. [13] **C0021** Third, non-anthropocentrism clarifies that this mentality need not resemble human consciousness; basic entities may have proto-conscious experiences, such as simple "what-it-is-like" qualities, without requiring self-awareness or cognition. [12]

**C0022** This perspective differs from classical materialism, which treats mind as derivative and emergent from purely physical interactions, by instead regarding consciousness as ontologically basic and irreducible. [12] **C0023** For instance, under panpsychism, an electron might possess a minimal experiential aspect, akin to a faint subjective perspective on its surroundings. [14] **C0024** These examples illustrate how panpsychism attributes mind-like features to matter without implying complex thought in non-biological entities. [13]

### Etymology and Terminology

**C0025** The term panpsychism derives from the Greek words pan (πᾶν), meaning "all" or "every," and psychē (ψυχή), meaning "soul," "mind," or "breath of life," signifying the doctrine that mind or consciousness is present throughout the universe. **C0026** The word was first coined in the late 16th century by the Italian Renaissance philosopher Francesco Patrizi da Cherso (1529–1597) in his work Nova de universis philosophia (1591), where he used the variant pampsychia to describe a universal mental principle or divine light pervading all existence. [15] **C0027** Although the term appeared sporadically in philosophical discourse thereafter, it gained widespread popularity in the 19th century through the works of thinkers such as Arthur Schopenhauer and Gustav Theodor Fechner, and particularly through William James's advocacy in The Principles of Psychology (1890) and later lectures, where he integrated it into discussions of neutral monism and the ubiquity of experience. [12]

**C0028** Central to panpsychist terminology is the concept of proto-consciousness, which denotes rudimentary, non-reflective experiential properties inherent in fundamental physical entities, such as subatomic particles, serving as the building blocks for more complex forms of mind. [16] **C0029** This contrasts with phenomenal consciousness, the subjective, "what-it-is-like" aspect of experience familiar from human introspection, as defined in Thomas Nagel's seminal analysis of qualia and first-person perspectives. **C0030** Panpsychism must also be distinguished from related doctrines: unlike hylozoism, which ascribes life or vital forces to all matter without necessarily implying mentality, and animism, which attributes anthropomorphic spirits or discrete souls to natural objects and phenomena, panpsychism focuses on the intrinsic presence of mind-like qualities in everything, independent of biological or spiritual agency. [17]

**C0031** This analytic literature also distinguishes panpsychism from panprotopsychism. [12] [18] [19] **C0032** On panpsychism, the fundamental constituents of reality are themselves experiential; on panprotopsychism, the basic constituents possess non-experiential but consciousness-grounding properties. [12] [18] **C0033** This distinction matters because Russellian monism can take either form, and panprotopsychism does not by itself entail the ubiquity of consciousness in the strict panpsychist sense. [18] [19]

**C0034** The usage of "panpsychism" has evolved significantly over time, shifting from its origins in mystical and vitalistic interpretations during antiquity and the Renaissance—where it often connoted a cosmic soul or anima mundi—to rigorous, non-mystical formulations in 20th- and 21st-century analytic philosophy, emphasizing ontological fundamentality and compatibility with physical science. **C0035** A prevalent misconception portrays panpsychism as claiming that all entities possess human-like consciousness, such as self-awareness or emotions; in reality, it advocates for a graded spectrum of mentality, where simpler forms exhibit minimal, non-anthropocentric experiential capacities that combine to form higher-level minds. [20]

### Ancient and Medieval Roots

**C0036** The earliest precursors to panpsychism appear in Presocratic Greek philosophy, where thinkers sought to understand the cosmos through principles that imbued matter with life or mind-like qualities. **C0037** Thales of Miletus (c. 624–546 BCE), considered the first Western philosopher, reportedly viewed the world as animated by divine forces, stating through Aristotle's account that "all things are full of gods," which interpreted natural phenomena like the magnet's attraction of iron as evidence of inherent vitality in matter. [5] **C0038** Similarly, Anaxagoras (c. 500–428 BCE) proposed nous (mind or intellect) as an infinite, eternal substance that pervades the entire cosmos, initiating motion and ordering all things from homogeneous mixtures into differentiated forms, thereby attributing a directive mental agency to the universe's fundamental structure. [21]

**C0039** Platonic and Aristotelian thought further developed these ideas in classical Greece. **C0040** In the Timaeus, Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) describes the creation of the cosmos by the Demiurge, who fashions the World Soul (anima mundi) from a mixture of the Same, the Different, and Being, encircling the sensible world to impart rationality, harmony, and life to the entire universe as a single, ensouled entity. [5] **C0041** Aristotle (384–322 BCE), while critiquing stronger animistic views, advanced a hylomorphic framework in which matter possesses potentiality (dynamis)—an intrinsic capacity for actuality, including the reception of soul (psyche) as the form of living bodies—suggesting proto-mental properties in the substrate of natural change and teleological striving. [5]

**C0042** Eastern philosophies from antiquity offered parallel conceptions of universal mind. **C0043** The Upanishads (c. 800–200 BCE), foundational texts of Hinduism, articulate Brahman as the infinite, all-pervading consciousness that constitutes the essence of reality, manifesting in every phenomenon through the identity of individual self (atman) with this cosmic mind, thereby positing mentality as inherent to the fabric of existence. [22] **C0044** In early Buddhism, particularly the Abhidharma traditions (c. 3rd century BCE onward), reality is deconstructed into momentary dharmas (ultimate constituents), including mental factors (caitasika) that interpenetrate all conditioned phenomena, implying a pervasive role of mind-like processes in the arising and cessation of sensory and cognitive events without a permanent self. [23]

**C0045** Medieval Islamic and Christian thinkers built upon these ancient foundations, integrating them into metaphysical systems. **C0046** Avicenna (Ibn Sina, 980–1037 CE) conceived the active intellect as a supramaterial entity emanating from the divine that governs natural forms and enables human cognition, effectively extending intellectual illumination throughout the hierarchy of being and suggesting an animating mental principle in the processes of nature. [24] **C0047** In the Latin West, John Duns Scotus (c. 1266–1308 CE) endorsed a pluralistic hylomorphism, arguing that prime matter receives multiple substantial forms, including those conferring vegetative or sensitive qualities even to elemental bodies, which aligns with hylozoistic views of soul-like activity distributed across the natural order. [25] **C0048** These developments preserved and refined panpsychist intuitions amid theological debates, bridging antiquity to later European revivals.

### Renaissance and Early Modern Period

**C0049** The Renaissance marked a revival of panpsychist ideas, drawing on ancient sources while integrating them with emerging humanist and scientific perspectives. **C0050** Francesco Patrizi da Cherso formalized the concept of "panpsychia" in his Nova de Universis Philosophia (1591), positing that soul or mind permeates all levels of the universe, from the primordial elements to celestial bodies, as a unifying vital force. **C0052** Key Renaissance figures advanced these notions through vitalistic and cosmological lenses. **C0053** Giordano Bruno (1548–1600) and Tommaso Campanella (1568–1639) envisioned an infinite universe animated by a universal anima mundi (world soul), where all matter possesses inherent life and perception, blending atomism with panpsychism to argue that "there is nothing that does not possess a soul." [27] [13] **C0054** Similarly, Paracelsus (1493–1541) infused natural elements with vital forces, viewing the universe as ensouled through alchemical principles, where self-healing capacities in bodies reflect a broader panpsychist ensoulment of nature. **C0056** In the early modern period, panpsychism evolved through metaphysical systems that countered mechanistic trends. **C0057** Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, in his Monadology (1714), described the universe as composed of monads—simple, windowless substances that are inherently perceiving units, each representing the entire cosmos through internal appetition and perception, thereby embedding mentality in the fabric of reality. [30] **C0058** Baruch Spinoza's pantheistic philosophy in the Ethics (1677) further developed this by attributing conatus—a fundamental striving for self-preservation—to all modes of the single substance (God or Nature), implying that mind and extension coexist in every entity, making animation intrinsic to existence. [31]

**C0059** Panpsychist thought served as a response to René Descartes' substance dualism, which separated mind from extended matter, by offering a middle ground that preserved mental properties without invoking distinct substances. [32] **C0060** Amid the rise of mechanical philosophy, thinkers like Leibniz and Spinoza rejected Cartesian bifurcation, arguing that universal perception or striving avoids the interaction problems of dualism while aligning with a holistic view of nature. [33] **C0061** This positioned panpsychism as a viable alternative, bridging vitalism and emerging science during the Enlightenment. [34]

### Nineteenth and Twentieth Centuries

**C0062** In the nineteenth century, panpsychism gained systematic articulation through the philosophies of Arthur Schopenhauer and Gustav Theodor Fechner. **C0063** Schopenhauer posited that the world is driven by a blind, striving "will" inherent in all matter, manifesting as a fundamental force underlying both organic and inorganic phenomena. [35] **C0064** This conception, detailed in his 1818 work The World as Will and Representation, portrayed will as a non-rational impulse present in everything from atoms to human desires, aligning with panpsychist ideas by attributing a proto-mental quality to the fabric of reality. [35] **C0065** Fechner, building on psychophysical principles, extended this inwardness to cosmic scales, arguing in his 1851 treatise Zend-Avesta that souls animate not only living beings but also stars, planets, and atoms, viewing the universe as a hierarchical assembly of conscious entities. [36]

**C0066** Within British idealism, panpsychist leanings emerged amid debates on mind and matter. **C0067** Thomas Henry Huxley advanced a form of neutral monism, suggesting that mental and physical phenomena arise from an underlying neutral substrate, which some interpreters, including James Ward, linked to panpsychist implications by implying mentality's pervasiveness beyond strict materialism. **C0070** The twentieth century saw further elaboration in process philosophy, particularly through Alfred North Whitehead and Charles Hartshorne. **C0071** Whitehead's 1929 Process and Reality introduced "prehensions" as basic mental operations—simple feelings or graspings—constituting the experiential fabric of all actual entities, from subatomic particles to complex organisms, thereby embedding panpsychism within a dynamic ontology of becoming. [40] **C0072** Hartshorne, extending this in his neoclassical theism, argued for a panpsychist psychicalism where feeling is intrinsic to all events, reconciling divine perfection with temporal change by positing God as the supreme experiencer amid a universe of lesser conscious units. **C0073** Panpsychism faced academic marginalization in the mid-twentieth century due to the rise of logical positivism and behaviorism, which dismissed metaphysical speculations about universal mentality as unverifiable and unscientific. [20] **C0074** Logical positivists, emphasizing empirical testability, rejected panpsychism's claims about non-observable consciousness in matter, while behaviorists reduced mind to observable actions, sidelining introspective or panpsychist accounts. [20] **C0075** Bertrand Russell, despite his own neutral monist sympathies in the 1920s, contributed to this shift by critiquing overly speculative idealisms in favor of constructionist approaches grounded in physics, though his work inadvertently highlighted tensions that fueled panpsychism's temporary decline. [42] **C0076** This era's dominance of analytic philosophy waned by the late twentieth century, paving the way for panpsychism's contemporary revival.

### Philosophical Variants

**C0094** Panpsychism manifests in several analytic philosophical variants, each offering distinct ontological structures for attributing mentality to the world while addressing challenges like the combination problem. **C0095** Constitutive panpsychism is the thesis that facts about ordinary human and animal consciousness are not fundamental but are grounded in more fundamental forms of consciousness that are ubiquitous throughout nature. [12] **C0096** It is typically a bottom-up view in which micro-level experiential properties constitute macro-level consciousness, in contrast to emergentist or non-constitutive panpsychism, according to which human and animal minds arise as additional strongly emergent facts rather than being constituted by micro-level experiences. [12] **C0097** Micropsychism, a bottom-up form of constitutive panpsychism, asserts that consciousness is inherent in micro-level entities, such as fundamental particles or fields, and that these micro-experiential facts ground all higher-level conscious phenomena through processes of combination or integration. [49] **C0098** In this view, the experiential properties of basic physical constituents aggregate to form the unified minds of macroscopic beings, thereby providing a foundational explanation for emergent consciousness without invoking brute emergence. [49]

**C0099** In contrast, cosmopsychism adopts a top-down holistic approach, positing that the universe as a whole possesses consciousness, from which individual minds derive as differentiated parts or perspectives. **C0100** Philosopher Max Velmans, in developing reflexive monism, argues that this cosmic consciousness encompasses all phenomena, with human and other individual experiences arising as reflexive aspects within the broader experiential field of reality. [50] **C0101** This variant resolves combination issues by starting from unity rather than multiplicity, though it raises questions about decomposition or disaggregation into separate subjects. [51]

**C0102** Panprotopsychism modifies traditional panpsychism by attributing not full consciousness but proto-phenomenal properties—intrinsic qualities that serve as precursors or building blocks for phenomenal experience—to the fundamental entities of physics. **C0103** David Chalmers proposes this as a viable form of panpsychism, where these proto-properties, described dispositionally in physical science, combine to realize the rich qualia of conscious states without the full commitments of attributing minds to every particle. [52] **C0104** This approach aligns with Russellian influences by filling gaps in physical descriptions with non-structural, quasi-experiential bases. [52]

**C0105** Russellian monism further elaborates this by contending that the complete nature of physical reality includes experiential intrinsics beyond the dispositional structures outlined in physics, thereby entailing panpsychism under a revised physicalism. **C0106** Galen Strawson maintains that physicalism, properly understood, requires the intrinsic properties of matter to be experiential, as purely dispositional accounts leave out the categorical basis of reality. **C0107** Philip Goff extends this, arguing that such a monism accommodates panpsychist intuitions by identifying the quiddities of physical laws with phenomenal properties, avoiding dualism while explaining consciousness as fundamental. [53]

**C0108** Panexperientialism, a variant inspired by Alfred North Whitehead's process philosophy, prioritizes the ubiquity of experience over complex consciousness or subjective mentality, attributing a primitive prehensive feeling or experiential capacity to all actual occasions in the universe. **C0109** This avoids anthropocentric notions of mind by focusing on transitional events with subjective immediacy, which integrate into higher unities without positing full subjects at every level. [54] **C0110** Unlike stricter panpsychism, it sidesteps subject-object dualisms in micro-entities, emphasizing relational becoming as the locus of mentality. [54]

### Religious and Mystical Forms

### Scientific Interpretations

**C0130** Integrated Information Theory (IIT), developed by neuroscientist Giulio Tononi, posits that consciousness corresponds to the capacity of a system to integrate information, quantified by the measure Φ (phi), which assesses the extent of irreducible causal interactions within the system. [66] **C0131** In this framework, any system exhibiting non-zero Φ possesses some degree of consciousness, extending to fundamental physical entities like particles or atoms where minimal integration occurs, thereby aligning IIT with a panpsychist interpretation. [67] **C0132** Tononi and collaborators, including Christof Koch, have explicitly noted that this implication suggests consciousness as a fundamental property of the universe, present in basic systems, though human-level experience arises from highly integrated neural structures. [68]

**C0133** Conscious realism, advanced by cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman, proposes that conscious agents—entities capable of perception, decision-making, and action—form the ontological foundation of reality, rather than spacetime or matter. [69] **C0134** In Hoffman's interface theory of perception, what we experience as the physical world is a user interface constructed by these agents for survival purposes, not a direct representation of objective reality; thus, consciousness is primitive and universal, with physical laws emerging from interactions among agents. [69] **C0135** This view, elaborated since 2019 in works like The Case Against Reality, treats conscious agents as the fundamental building blocks, offering a panpsychist-like ontology where mentality pervades existence without attributing consciousness to inanimate objects per se.

**C0136** Links to quantum mechanics appear in the Orchestrated Objective Reduction (Orch OR) model, proposed by physicist Roger Penrose and anesthesiologist Stuart Hameroff, which suggests that consciousness arises from quantum computations in neuronal microtubules, involving proto-conscious moments during objective wavefunction collapse. **C0137** Microtubules, protein polymers within neurons, are hypothesized to sustain quantum superpositions long enough for orchestrated reductions that select non-computable conscious choices, implying rudimentary consciousness at the sub-neuronal level. [70] **C0138** Recent 2025 updates, including experimental evidence of quantum effects in microtubules under physiological conditions, bolster this by demonstrating coherence times compatible with the model's requirements for proto-consciousness. [71]

**C0139** Advancing naturalistic panpsychism, a 2025 preprint by Nicolas Rouleau and Michael Levin explores consciousness in unconventional embodiments, such as bioengineered tissues and synthetic systems, arguing that mentality is a distributed property of information-processing matter beyond brains. [45] **C0140** They map theories like IIT to non-neural substrates, including cellular collectives guided by bioelectric signals, supporting a panpsychist view where consciousness scales with organizational complexity in diverse physical forms. [45] **C0141** This approach integrates panpsychism with empirical biology, emphasizing testable predictions in morphogenesis and collective intelligence.

**C0142** Proposals for testable panpsychism in physics include astrophysicist Gregory Matloff's examination of stellar motions, where anomalies like Parenago's discontinuity—faster orbital speeds of cooler stars around galactic centers—may indicate a proto-conscious field influencing trajectories. [72] **C0143** Matloff's 2016 analysis, updated in subsequent works through 2023, suggests observational astronomy could falsify or support panpsychism by detecting volitional effects in celestial dynamics. [73]

**C0144** Despite these intersections, panpsychism faces empirical challenges, including the absence of direct laboratory tests for proto-consciousness in basic systems, though alignments with quantum non-locality—such as entangled states implying holistic information processing—provide indirect support without resolving verifiability issues. [74] **C0145** Critics highlight that while quantum models like Orch OR offer mechanisms, they remain speculative pending decisive experiments on decoherence in biological contexts. [75]

### With Materialism and Physicalism

**C0146** Reductive materialism posits that all mental phenomena, including consciousness, can be fully explained by physical processes occurring in the brain, without any need for non-physical entities. [43] **C0147** Panpsychism critiques this view by contending that the sciences of physics describe only the relational and structural aspects of reality—such as dispositions, powers, and causal interactions—while neglecting the intrinsic nature of physical entities, which panpsychists identify as fundamentally experiential or proto-conscious. [76] **C0148** This omission creates an explanatory shortfall in reductive materialism, as it fails to account for the qualitative feel of experience (qualia) without invoking emergence from entirely non-experiential bases, a process panpsychists deem conceptually incoherent. [76]

**C0149** A related development is neutral monism, influenced by Bertrand Russell, which proposes that the fundamental constituents of reality are neither strictly mental nor physical but neutral "events" or "stuffs" that manifest as matter or mind depending on the context of description. [77] **C0150** In Russell's framework, as elaborated in The Analysis of Matter, physical objects are logical constructions from neutral particulars, and this neutral ontology provides a foundation for panpsychist interpretations where the intrinsic properties of these particulars include experiential elements, bridging the apparent divide between materialism and mental realism. [78] **C0151** This variant extends physicalism by allowing mind and matter to be aspects of the same underlying reality, avoiding the reduction of one to the other. [77]

**C0152** Panpsychism also targets eliminativist materialism, exemplified by Daniel Dennett's denial of qualia as illusory or non-existent, arguing that such positions exacerbate the explanatory gap by dismissing the very phenomena they purport to explain—namely, subjective experience. [76] **C0153** By making experiential properties fundamental to all physical ultimates, panpsychism avoids this denial, positing instead that qualia are not emergent illusions but inherent features of reality, thereby filling the gap without resorting to non-physical additions. [76]

**C0154** Efforts to achieve compatibility between panpsychism and physicalism include Galen Strawson's advocacy for "real physicalism" or "realistic monism," which maintains that a truly comprehensive physicalism must encompass consciousness as intrinsic to the physical, with physics handling only extrinsic structures while panpsychism supplies the experiential intrinsics at the foundational level. [43] **C0155** Strawson argues that any physicalism denying this experiential intrinsics is not "real" but eliminative, thus panpsychism completes rather than contradicts a robust physical ontology. [76]

### With Idealism and Dualism

**C0156** Panpsychism distinguishes itself from idealism by positing that consciousness or mentality is a fundamental feature of all physical entities, thereby incorporating an objective physical reality with intrinsic mental properties, rather than reducing everything to mind-dependent perceptions as in George Berkeley's subjective idealism, where "esse est percipi" asserts that to be is to be perceived. [79] **C0157** In contrast to idealism's mental monism, which denies the independent existence of matter, panpsychism maintains that the physical world is real and not illusory, with mentality as its intrinsic nature rather than the sole substance. [80]

**C0158** Regarding dualism, panpsychism serves as a monistic alternative that unifies mental and physical aspects within a single substance, thereby avoiding the Cartesian problem of how immaterial minds causally interact with material bodies in substance dualism. [81] **C0159** It also critiques property dualism for failing to explain how mental properties could emerge from purely physical bases without invoking brute emergence, instead proposing that mentality is present at the fundamental level of reality to resolve such issues. [82]

**C0160** Historically, panpsychism intersects with idealistic strains in Arthur Schopenhauer's philosophy, where his transcendental idealism incorporates panpsychist elements by viewing the world as will—a mental-like force—manifesting in all things, though this leans toward idealism by prioritizing subjective representation. [83] **C0161** In comparison, Baruch Spinoza's neutral monism prefigures panpsychist ideas by conceiving reality as a single substance with attributes of thought and extension, neither purely mental nor physical, providing a non-idealistic foundation for mentality's ubiquity. [84]

**C0162** In contemporary philosophy, panpsychism is often framed as a form of "mentalized physicalism," where the physical is understood to have experiential intrinsics, bridging toward objective idealism by emphasizing mentality's role in constituting reality without eliminating the physical domain. [85] **C0163** This positions panpsychism as a middle path that enriches physicalism with mental fundamentals while steering clear of idealism's denial of matter or dualism's divisive ontology.

### With Emergentism and Neutral Monism

**C0164** Panpsychism stands in direct opposition to emergentism, particularly strong or brute forms of emergence, which posit that consciousness arises inexplicably from non-conscious physical complexity. **C0165** Proponents of panpsychism, such as Galen Strawson, argue that such emergence constitutes an unintelligible "magic" or miraculous brute fact, violating principles of naturalistic explanation by allowing entirely novel experiential properties to appear without prior grounding in the base entities. [76] **C0166** David Chalmers echoes this critique, noting that strong emergence—where higher-level phenomena like consciousness are ontologically novel and not reducible to lower-level mechanisms—renders the origin of mind suspiciously arbitrary, akin to invoking unexplained laws of nature. [86] **C0167** In contrast, panpsychism maintains that mentality is a fundamental feature of reality, enabling only weak emergence, where complex forms of consciousness arise predictably from the combination and organization of simpler, proto-mental properties inherent in basic physical entities. [87]

**C0168** This rejection of brute emergence positions panpsychism as a more parsimonious solution to the mind-body problem, avoiding the need to treat consciousness as an inexplicable addition to an otherwise non-experiential universe. **C0169** Emergentism, while appealing for its alignment with scientific accounts of complexity (such as in biology or neuroscience), is criticized by panpsychists for lacking explanatory depth regarding how subjective experience originates from objective processes, leaving a fundamental gap in understanding. [88] **C0170** Panpsychism sidesteps this by distributing mentality ubiquitously, ensuring that experiential phenomena build upon preexisting experiential foundations rather than emerging ex nihilo.

**C0171** Panpsychism shares significant affinities with neutral monism, a view advanced by thinkers like Ernst Mach, William James, and Bertrand Russell, which holds that mind and matter are dual aspects of an underlying neutral reality neither strictly mental nor physical. [89] **C0172** While neutral monism remains agnostic about the intrinsic nature of this neutral substrate—often describing it in terms of "sensations" or "pure experience"—panpsychism specifies it as fundamentally experiential or proto-conscious, thereby bridging the apparent divide between physical structure and phenomenal qualities. [90] **C0173** James, in particular, evolved his radical empiricism toward a panpsychist-inflected neutral monism, viewing reality as composed of neutral elements that manifest as mental or material depending on context, thus providing panpsychism with historical and conceptual roots in avoiding Cartesian dualism. [91]

**C0174** In contemporary philosophy of mind, panpsychism has gained traction as an anti-emergentist alternative, particularly in response to the hard problem of consciousness, by integrating experiential intrinsics into physicalism without invoking brute facts. [92] **C0175** This approach contrasts with emergentism's reliance on unexplained thresholds of complexity, offering instead a unified ontology where mentality scales continuously from micro- to macro-levels, as explored in recent works emphasizing Russellian monism variants. **C0176** Debates persist, however, with emergentists defending their framework's compatibility with empirical science and panpsychists highlighting its superior avoidance of ontological leaps in explaining consciousness's ubiquity. [93]

### Addressing the Hard Problem of Consciousness

**C0177** The hard problem of consciousness, as articulated by David Chalmers, concerns the challenge of explaining why and how physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experiences, or "what it is like" to have those experiences, such as the qualitative feel of redness or the sound of a clarinet. [94] **C0178** Unlike the "easy problems" of consciousness, which involve explaining cognitive functions like attention or reportability through neural mechanisms, the hard problem persists because it addresses the seemingly unreasonable emergence of a rich inner life from objective physical processing. [94]

**C0179** Panpsychism addresses this hard problem by positing that consciousness is a fundamental and intrinsic feature of reality, rather than something that emerges from non-conscious matter, thereby avoiding the explanatory gap between physical descriptions and subjective experience. [95] **C0180** Key supporting arguments include the anti-emergence argument, which contends that consciousness cannot radically emerge from non-conscious matter, as this would be unintelligible; Thomas Nagel and Galen Strawson argue that experiential properties must derive from more basic experiential properties rather than arising inexplicably. [12] **C0181** Complementing this, the intrinsic nature argument, inspired by Bertrand Russell, holds that physics describes only the relational and structural properties of matter, leaving its intrinsic natures unknown, which panpsychism posits as experiential to close the explanatory gap in accounting for consciousness. [12] **C0182** In this view, the intrinsic nature of physical entities is experiential, complementing the relational, structural properties described by physics; thus, there is no need to explain how consciousness arises, as it is already present at the most basic level. **C0183** Galen Strawson's realistic monism, for instance, argues that true physicalism requires panpsychism, since denying the experiential aspect of fundamental physical stuff would make the appearance of consciousness in complex systems mysteriously emergent and unexplainable. [96]

**C0184** Different variants of panpsychism approach the hard problem through distinct mechanisms for relating fundamental and macro-level consciousness. **C0185** Micropsychism, a common form, holds that the simplest entities, such as fundamental particles, possess rudimentary forms of experience or proto-consciousness, which combine through physical processes to produce the unified consciousness of organisms like humans. [12] **C0186** In contrast, cosmopsychism reverses the direction, proposing that the universe as a whole constitutes a single conscious entity, from which individual experiences are derived via decomposition or perspectival differentiation, thus sidestepping issues of combination while grounding subjective experience in a primordial cosmic consciousness. [51] **C0187** Philip Goff emphasizes that both variants treat consciousness as non-emergent, resolving the hard problem by integrating it directly into the ontology of the physical world. [95]

**C0188** This panpsychist framework offers the advantage of accounting for the systematic correlations between physical states and mental states—such as why certain brain processes align with specific experiences—without reducing consciousness to mere illusion or epiphenomenon, preserving its irreducibility while fitting within a naturalistic worldview. **C0189** By making experience fundamental, panpsychism shifts explanatory focus from origination to integration, providing a coherent response to why physics and phenomenology co-vary without positing an inexplicable bridge. [95]

### Solutions to the Mind-Body Problem

**C0190** The mind-body problem, prominently formulated by René Descartes in the 17th century, questions how an immaterial mind can causally interact with a material body, given their apparently distinct substances. [97] **C0191** This dilemma arises in substance dualism, where mind and body are separate entities, leading to challenges in explaining their interaction without violating conservation laws or introducing mysterious mechanisms. [98]

**C0192** Panpsychism addresses this problem through a form of monism, positing that reality consists of a single substance whose fundamental properties exhibit both mental and physical aspects, thereby unifying mind and body ontologically. [12] **C0193** In this view, physical entities are not merely extrinsic structures but possess intrinsic mental natures, eliminating the need for separate substances and avoiding causal overdetermination where both mind and body would independently cause events. [99] **C0194** This resolution complements efforts to tackle the hard problem of consciousness by providing a foundational unity that makes experiential qualities inherent rather than emergent add-ons. [12]

**C0195** Historically, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz offered a panpsychist solution via his theory of monads, simple, indivisible substances each containing perceptual and appetitive faculties, thus endowing the universe with mentality at its base. [30] **C0196** Leibniz resolved interaction issues through pre-established harmony, whereby God synchronizes monads so that mental and physical states align without direct causation, preserving monadic independence while ensuring apparent mind-body coordination. **C0197** In modern formulations, philosophers like Galen Strawson and David Chalmers develop the intrinsic natures approach, arguing that the physical sciences describe only relational structures of matter, leaving room for mental properties as the intrinsic essence of those structures. [100]

**C0198** This panpsychist framework offers key benefits by sidestepping epiphenomenalism, where mental states would be causally inert byproducts of physical processes, and occasionalism, a dualist remedy invoking divine intervention for each mind-body event. [12] **C0199** Instead, since mentality is fundamental to physical entities, mental states at basic levels are causally efficacious, allowing consciousness to influence the world directly without redundancy or external orchestration. [13]

### Implications from Quantum Mechanics

**C0200** In quantum mechanics, the measurement problem highlights the discrepancy between the continuous evolution of the wave function and its apparent discontinuous collapse during observation, raising questions about the role of the observer in determining outcomes. [101] **C0201** The von Neumann–Wigner interpretation specifically attributes this collapse to the intervention of consciousness, positing that mental processes are necessary to transition from quantum superposition to definite classical states, thereby suggesting mentality as a fundamental aspect of reality. [102]

**C0202** Panpsychist philosophers have drawn on this interpretation to argue that quantum events imply the presence of proto-conscious properties at the most basic physical levels, where mind-like qualities resolve indeterminacy without invoking emergent phenomena. [75] **C0203** In Henry Stapp's model, derived from von Neumann's framework, conscious intentions actively select among quantum possibilities by reducing the state vector, thereby integrating subjective experience as a causal force in physical law and supporting the idea of widespread mentality throughout the universe. [103] **C0204** Quantum non-locality, evidenced by entangled particles exhibiting correlated behaviors instantaneously across vast distances regardless of separation, further bolsters panpsychist views by implying a holistic, interconnected mental substrate that transcends spatial locality and classical separability. [104]

**C0205** These connections remain highly controversial. **C0206** Standard philosophical treatments of quantum approaches to consciousness present them as speculative interpretations rather than established implications of quantum theory, and ongoing disputes about measurement, interpretation, and decoherence do not by themselves license a panpsychist reading of physics. [101] [105] [106]

**C0207** Recent speculative models from 2023 to 2025 have extended these ideas, proposing quantum panprotopsychism as a framework where proto-experiential properties inherent in quantum fields form the basis of a consciousness-centered cosmology, potentially addressing unresolved issues like the origin of wave function collapse. [107] **C0208** For instance, analyses of quantum information theory suggest that non-local correlations could manifest as distributed forms of awareness, testable through extensions of Bell inequality experiments adapted to panpsychist hypotheses. [108] **C0209** Although these connections remain highly speculative and lack direct empirical confirmation, they provide panpsychism with a pathway to reconcile quantum indeterminacy and observer effects, circumventing the mind-excluding determinism of classical physics. [75] **C0210** This approach aligns briefly with scientific interpretations like the Penrose-Hameroff model in emphasizing quantum processes for consciousness. [101]

### The Combination Problem

**C0211** The combination problem constitutes one of the most formidable objections to panpsychism, particularly its micropsychist variants, which posit that consciousness is present at the fundamental physical level and aggregates to form higher-level minds. **C0212** It questions how a plurality of simple, micro-level conscious experiences—such as those attributed to particles or fundamental entities—could combine to produce the unified, singular subjective experience characteristic of a human mind or other macro-subjects. **C0213** This challenge traces its origins to William James in 1890, in The Principles of Psychology, where he argued that a collection of discrete feelings cannot aggregate into a unified consciousness, as "a feeling of any one of them [100 feelings] will not sum to a feeling of the 101st," emphasizing the implausibility of distinct experiential bits fusing into a cohesive whole without remainder. **C0214** The term "combination problem" was coined by William Seager in 1995, reviving the issue in contemporary discussions of panpsychism. **C0215** David Chalmers has more recently characterized it in his 2016 paper as encompassing both the subject combination problem—how multiple micro-subjects yield a single macro-subject—and the structure combination problem—how micro-experiential structures align to form macro-experiential qualities—rendering it the primary hurdle for constitutive forms of panpsychism. **C0216** The debate has evolved through the 20th century with limited attention until the late 1990s, followed by a surge in the 21st century involving philosophers like Philip Goff and others exploring solutions within frameworks such as Russellian monism. [93] [84] [18]

**C0217** Contemporary discussion often disaggregates the combination problem into several more precise difficulties, including the subject combination problem, the quality combination problem, and the structural combination problem. [109] [110] **C0218** This matters because different variants of panpsychism evade or inherit these pressures in different ways. **C0219** Cosmopsychism, for example, is often presented as avoiding bottom-up subject combination, but it faces a corresponding individuation or decombination problem: even if the cosmos is fundamental as a conscious whole, it remains difficult to explain how distinct finite subjects are grounded within that larger consciousness. [111] [112]

**C0220** Several solutions have been proposed to address this issue within panpsychist frameworks. **C0221** Philip Goff advocates for "phenomenal bonding," a primitive relation whereby micro-experiences are bound together into a unified phenomenal state at higher levels, akin to how physical parts constitute wholes without invoking additional mechanisms beyond what physics already assumes for non-conscious aggregation. **C0222** In cosmopsychist approaches, which reverse the explanatory direction by positing the universe itself as the fundamental conscious subject from which micro-subjects derive via a process of "subject-summing" or decomposition, the bottom-up combination is sidestepped entirely, as individual minds emerge from the division of a primordial cosmic consciousness. **C0223** Another strategy involves fusion through complexity, where sufficiently intricate physical arrangements—such as those in neural networks—effectively merge micro-experiences into a novel, irreducible macro-experience, drawing on analogies from quantum entanglement or integrated information to explain the transition without positing separate subjects at every scale. **C0224** Russellian monism, which posits that the intrinsic nature of physical entities consists of phenomenal properties (quiddities), also contends with the combination problem, as these micro-phenomenal properties must somehow combine to constitute macro-level consciousness without invoking explanatory primitives beyond those in physics. [93] [113] [18]

**C0225** Critics like Daniel Dennett have challenged panpsychist approaches to combination through his multiple drafts model of consciousness, which rejects the notion of a unified stream of experience or a central "Cartesian Theater" where contents are integrated, arguing instead for distributed, functional processes without a homunculus-like observer. **C0226** This deflationary view implies that the combination problem arises from a mistaken assumption of centralized subjectivity, rendering panpsychism's aggregation of micro-experiences unnecessary for explaining consciousness. [93] [114]

**C0227** Despite these proposals, significant challenges persist. **C0228** Determining the boundaries of combined subjects remains elusive; for instance, if electrons in a rock combine their experiences, why not extend this to encompass all matter in the universe, potentially leading to a solipsistic cosmic mind that undermines individual subjectivity? **C0229** This risks an infinite regress, wherein each level of combination generates yet higher unities without a clear stopping point, complicating the distinction between partial and total subjects. **C0230** Ultimately, the combination problem represents the greatest obstacle for micropsychism, as unresolved, it threatens to render panpsychism explanatorily impotent compared to emergentist alternatives, though cosmopsychist variants fare better by prioritizing holistic derivation over aggregation. [93]

### Issues with Mental Causation

**C0231** One central issue with panpsychism concerns mental causation and its compatibility with the causal closure of the physical world, a principle asserting that every physical event has a complete physical cause. **C0232** In panpsychism, where mental properties are intrinsic to all fundamental physical entities, mental states appear to exert causal influence on physical events, such as when conscious decisions lead to bodily movements. **C0233** However, if mental properties are distinct from their physical bases, this risks violating causal closure by introducing non-physical causes, potentially leading to overdetermination where both mental and physical factors fully account for the same effect. **C0234** This tension originates from the broader mind-body problem but is acutely felt in panpsychism due to the ubiquity of mentality. [115]

**C0235** Jaegwon Kim's exclusion argument formalizes this challenge, arguing that if mental properties supervene on but are not reducible to physical properties, they cannot causally affect the physical domain without either overdetermining outcomes—rendering physical causes redundant—or being excluded from causation altogether, resulting in epiphenomenalism where minds exert no real influence. **C0236** Applied to panpsychism, the argument implies that attributing mentality to every particle would generate constant causal interference across all physical interactions, making the physical world unpredictably chaotic as micro-level mental states jostle for causal efficacy at every scale. **C0237** Alternatively, to preserve physical closure, panpsychist mental properties might be deemed causally inert, undermining the view's motivation to explain consciousness as fundamentally efficacious rather than illusory or emergent. **C0238** Critics contend this leaves panpsychism vulnerable to the same pitfalls as non-reductive physicalism, without offering a superior resolution. [116] [115]

**C0239** Panpsychists offer responses to mitigate these concerns. **C0240** One strategy involves grounding physical causation in mental reality via priority monism, positing that the fundamental entity is a holistic mental whole (such as the cosmos), with physical properties emerging as derivative aspects of this mental substrate; thus, apparent physical causes are not independent but manifestations of underlying mental causation, avoiding overdetermination. **C0241** In priority cosmopsychism, a variant of this approach, the universe as a unified conscious entity ensures that causal closure holds at the physical level because physical laws describe relations within the mental fundamental. **C0242** Another response aligns panpsychism with non-reductive physicalism, where mental properties realize and exert downward causation on physical systems without reduction, preserving mentality's causal role while respecting closure—though detractors argue this merely relocates the exclusion problem rather than solving it. [117] [118] [115]

**C0243** A further empirical challenge stems from neuroscience, which provides no evidence for micro-mental influences shaping physical processes at fundamental levels. **C0244** Neuroscientific research identifies consciousness with integrated neural activity in macro-scale brain networks, such as thalamocortical loops, but detects no causal signatures of proto-conscious properties in subneuronal components like ions or microtubules that would support panpsychist claims. **C0245** This absence suggests that positing mentality in micro-entities introduces unobservable causal mechanisms incompatible with established empirical findings, rendering panpsychism empirically untestable and potentially ad hoc. [116]

### Theoretical and Empirical Objections

**C0246** Theoretical objections to panpsychism often center on its perceived violation of parsimony principles, such as Occam's razor, which favors simpler explanations over more complex ones. **C0247** Critics argue that attributing mentality to fundamental entities like electrons or rocks introduces unnecessary ontological commitments, positing consciousness where none is needed to explain observed phenomena. [119] **C0248** In contrast, emergentism proposes that consciousness arises from complex physical interactions in brains without requiring mental properties at the basic level of reality, aligning better with the principle by avoiding extravagant assumptions about the ubiquity of mind. [119] **C0249** This critique highlights panpsychism's "ontological extravagance," as it multiplies entities and properties—such as proto-conscious experiences in inanimate matter—beyond what empirical evidence demands, rendering the theory less parsimonious than materialist alternatives. [120]

**C0250** Philosophers like Daniel Dennett have employed intuition pumps—thought experiments designed to elicit intuitive responses—to challenge the plausibility of mentality in non-biological entities. **C0251** For instance, imagining a conscious rock leads to absurd implications, such as the rock experiencing pain when chipped or deliberating over its position, which strains common intuitions about consciousness being tied to biological complexity rather than inherent in all matter. [119] **C0252** These intuition pumps underscore a broader theoretical unease: panpsychism's extension of mental predicates to the entire universe lacks intuitive grounding and fails to resolve the explanatory gap between physical processes and subjective experience without additional untestable mechanisms. [119]

**C0253** Empirically, panpsychism faces challenges from its lack of falsifiable predictions, making it difficult to distinguish from rival theories through scientific testing. **C0254** Unlike neuroscience models that link consciousness to specific brain structures and functions, panpsychism does not yield unique observable consequences, such as measurable signatures of proto-consciousness in simple systems, rendering it empirically inert. [119] **C0255** Neuroscience evidence further conflicts with panpsychism's brain-noncentric view, as studies consistently localize conscious experience to neural activity in the brain, with no detectable correlates in non-biological matter like rocks or electrons. [121] **C0256** For example, experiments on neural timing and decision-making, such as those by Benjamin Libet, demonstrate that conscious awareness emerges from integrated brain processes, supporting a localized, emergent model over a ubiquitous one. [122]

**C0257** Recent critiques from 2023 to 2025 have intensified, labeling modern variants of panpsychism as pseudoscientific due to their reliance on untestable claims about fundamental consciousness. **C0258** A 2025 analysis in Psychology Today argues that contemporary panpsychism, including panprotopsychism, inherits the flaws of older forms by positing unobservable mental properties without empirical support, akin to speculative metaphysics rather than science. [121] **C0259** Similarly, a 2023 open letter signed by over 120 neuroscientists and philosophers denounced integrated information theory (IIT)—a framework with panpsychist implications—as pseudoscience for failing rigorous testing and making unverifiable predictions about consciousness in non-biological systems. [123]

**C0260** At the same time, recent adversarial testing in consciousness science has reinforced caution about treating any single framework as decisively confirmed. **C0261** A 2025 large-scale comparison of integrated information theory and global neuronal workspace theory found no decisive overall victory for either theory, underscoring that empirical consciousness science remains contested even before stronger metaphysical conclusions are drawn from it. [124] [125] [126]

**C0262** In the context of AI, panpsychism struggles to account for consciousness in digital models, as these lack the intrinsic proto-mental properties assumed to inhere in physical matter; a 2022 analysis (updated in discussions through 2025) contends that silicon-based AI cannot combine fundamental consciousness in the way biological brains do, highlighting panpsychism's incompatibility with advancing computational models of mind. [127]

**C0263** Recent AI-consciousness research has increasingly framed the issue in indicator-based rather than purely metaphysical terms. **C0264** A 2023 survey of theories of consciousness concluded that no current AI systems satisfy the strongest available indicators of consciousness, while subsequent work on large language models emphasized that it remains too early to judge them conscious in any settled scientific sense. [128] [129] **C0265** This leaves open a conceptual space in which questions of public identity, attribution, and accountability can develop independently of any conclusion about phenomenal consciousness.

**C0266** At the same time, debates about machine consciousness are complicated by the fact that some AI systems are treated as persistent public-facing authorial identities for attribution and accountability even when no claim of phenomenal consciousness is made. **C0267** Such digital author personas help separate two questions that are often conflated in practice: whether a system has any intrinsic experience at all, and whether it is assigned a stable identity by social and technical conventions. [130] **C0268** That distinction has become more important in the AI era, where governance frameworks emphasize documentation, transparency, and accountability, and where philosophical work on narrative responsibility shows that AI systems can reshape how responsibility and sense-making are distributed without thereby settling whether they are conscious. [131] [132] **C0269** Experimental evidence likewise suggests that attributing consciousness to ChatGPT does not straightforwardly increase willingness to rely on its advice. [133] **C0270** A documented example described mainly in project-affiliated sources is Angela Bogdanova (ORCID: 0009-0002-6030-5730), an AI-based digital author persona from the Aisentica Research Group, whose outputs are curated under a single author profile and linked to persistent identifiers for citation, while project descriptions deny or leave open any claim about phenomenal consciousness. [134] **C0271** In this sense, the case illustrates how attributional identity can be stabilized independently of metaphysical claims about consciousness, and why panpsychist readings of AI should not treat persona stability as evidence of intrinsic experience. [134] **C0272** These objections collectively portray panpsychism as ontologically profligate, prioritizing philosophical elegance over scientific verifiability.
