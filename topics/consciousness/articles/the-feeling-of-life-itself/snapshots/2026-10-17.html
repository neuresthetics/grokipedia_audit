The Feeling of Life Itself: Why Consciousness Is Widespread but Can't Be Computed is a 2019 book by the neuroscientist Christof Koch, published by The MIT Press.[1] The hardcover, ISBN 978-0-262-04281-9, appeared on 24 September 2019 and is listed at 280 pages; a paperback, ISBN 978-0-262-53955-5, followed on 8 September 2020, and an ebook, ISBN 978-0-262-35443-1, is dated 1 October 2019.[1][2][3] At publication Koch was president of the Allen Institute for Brain Science, which he had joined in 2011 as chief scientist after a professorship at the California Institute of Technology.[4] The book defines consciousness as any subjective experience, from the mundane to the exalted—the feeling of being alive—and outlines a quantitative theory based on integrated information, developed by the neuroscientist Giulio Tononi.[2][5] In that account, as Philip Ball summarized it, conscious experience has five properties, among them being unified, unique, and definite in content, and the physical requirement is a system's causal power to be acted on by its own past and to influence its own future.[5] The empirical setting is Koch's search, begun with Francis Crick, for the neural correlates of consciousness.[3][5] As the publisher states the argument, the theory expects the sights and sounds of experience in many, and perhaps all, animals, so consciousness would be more widespread than usually assumed.[2] It also holds that programmable computers, including a perfect software model of the brain, would lack consciousness, because a simulation does not have the causal structure of what it simulates: a simulated rainstorm is not wet inside.[3][2] Ball reports Koch locating the relevant feedback architecture toward the back of the cerebral cortex, and describes a clinical probe, drawn from Tononi's work, that sends magnetic pulses through the cortex and compares the response to a bell.[5] Reviews divided on the reach of those claims. Steven Rose, in Times Higher Education, called the project ambitious and was unconvinced by its panpsychism, on which consciousness might extend even to atoms, while galaxies are said not to qualify.[3] Peter Kassan, in Skeptical Inquirer, argued that a conventional survey of brain anatomy and neural correlates was joined to speculation about minds in bacteria, atoms, or cultured neurons, and that the five axioms and the measure φ do not explain how experience arises.[4] Ball treated the book as a challenge to the claim that machine consciousness is a short step from more computing power, and wrote that the picture remains one view among many, without a resolution among neuroscientists or philosophers.[5] Later tests left the theory the book defends unsettled. On 23 June 2023, at a meeting of the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness in New York, Koch conceded a 1998 bet with the philosopher David Chalmers that the neural mechanism of consciousness would be known by then.[6] Adversarial tests of integrated information theory against global workspace theory, presented at the same meeting, were inconclusive, and the two made a further bet for 2048.[7] In September 2023 a letter signed by 124 scholars, posted on PsyArXiv, called integrated information theory pseudoscience; Koch replied that the theory may be empirically wrong but states its assumptions clearly, and the neuroscientist Anil Seth called the pseudoscience label inflammatory.[8]

Background

Career and the search for neural correlates

The Feeling of Life Itself was written after Christof Koch had spent decades treating consciousness as a laboratory problem. He received a Ph.D. in 1982 from the Max Planck Institute for Biological Cybernetics in Tübingen, then spent four years as a postdoctoral fellow at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.[9] He joined the California Institute of Technology faculty in 1986 and stayed until 2013, latterly as the Lois and Victor Troendle Professor of Cognitive and Behavioral Biology.[9] In 2011 he became chief scientific officer of the Allen Institute for Brain Science, and in 2015 its president, the posts he held when the book appeared.[9] The program that defined that work was his collaboration with Francis Crick. Crick, a co-recipient of the 1962 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the structure of DNA, moved in 1976 to the Salk Institute for Biological Studies and turned to the brain.[10][11] Koch has dated their close work to 1988 through Crick’s death.[12] A 2004 profile described Crick at the Salk Institute and Koch, then a professor of computation and neural systems at Caltech, looking for the neuronal states associated with awareness rather than debating whether subjective qualities could be explained at all.[13] Their 1990 paper took visual awareness as the tractable case: a fast form linked to iconic memory, and a slower form in which attention binds an object’s features by coherent oscillations, probably around 40–70 Hz, that then engage short-term memory.[14] A 2003 review called the project a framework, not a finished theory, explaining the neural correlates of consciousness for vision by competing coalitions of neurons.[15] Koch’s institutional statement of the target is the minimal biophysical mechanisms jointly sufficient for any one specific conscious percept.[9] The collaboration also shaped his 2004 book The Quest for Consciousness.[13] Crick died on 28 July 2004; Koch told Science that Crick was still editing a manuscript on his deathbed.[10][11] Their final joint paper, published in 2005, asked whether the claustrum, a thin sheet of neurons linked to much of the cortex, contributes to integrated conscious percepts.[16] The correlate program had made consciousness an experimental subject without saying why one pattern of activity is experienced and another is not. In the 2004 profile, philosopher David Chalmers granted the correlations but distinguished them from an explanation.[13]

Collaboration with Tononi

Giulio Tononi approached the same gap from the structure of experience. Trained in medicine and psychiatry at Pisa, he worked from 1990 to 2000 at the Neurosciences Institute with Gerald Edelman, and later became professor of psychiatry at the University of Wisconsin–Madison.[17] In 1998 Tononi and Edelman proposed the dynamic core hypothesis: integration and complexity, not the activity of one region, characterize the neural processes behind experience.[18] Tononi’s 2004 paper identified consciousness with a capacity to integrate information. Differentiation, a large repertoire of possible experiences, and integration, the unity of each one, motivated Φ (phi), the causally effective information integrated across a subset’s weakest link.[19] He used that quantity to ask why the thalamocortical system is associated with experience while the cerebellum is not, and why consciousness falls in dreamless sleep even though neurons remain active.[19] A 2014 formulation by Masafumi Oizumi, Larissa Albantakis, and Tononi, integrated information theory 3.0, identified an experience with a maximally irreducible conceptual structure and its quantity with Φ-max.[20] Koch presented the theory to a general readership in 2009, treating Φ as integrated information from causal interactions and contrasting it with information merely stored, as on a disk, which the theory does not count as conscious.[21] With Tononi in 2015, he argued that correlates cannot by themselves explain the cortex–cerebellum difference or decide borderline cases—patients with little remaining cortex, infants, other species, and machines.[22] That paper, and a 2016 review with Melanie Boly and Marcello Massimini, derived the physical substrate of consciousness from phenomenal properties and described it as a maximum of intrinsic cause–effect power.[23] The Feeling of Life Itself, issued by MIT Press in 2019, is Koch’s book-length account of this framework.[9]

Contents

The Feeling of Life Itself, published by MIT Press in 2019, runs from a preface, “Consciousness Redux,” through fourteen chapters to a coda, “Why This Matters.”[34] Christof Koch presents integrated information theory, Giulio Tononi’s proposal, as an argument that starts from experience and then states consequences for animals, patients, and machines.[35] Peter Kassan’s review treats the brain chapters as closer to mainstream neuroscience than those consequences.[4]

Experience, animals, and the brain

“What Is Consciousness?” defines consciousness as experience itself. Koch ends the chapter with five features he later calls axioms: every experience “exists for itself, is structured, is the specific way it is, is one, and is definite.”[36] Steven Rose describes that object as a freeze-frame of perception and mood, interrupted only by sleep or anesthesia.[3] “Who Is Conscious?” and “Animal Consciousness” proceed by similarity. Other people are treated as uncontroversial; mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, and invertebrates, including honey bees and octopuses, are then placed on one scale, with no Rubicon—Koch cites Charles Darwin on earthworms—between sentient and non-sentient animals, and with richness said to thin as nervous systems simplify.[37] Alun Anderson’s review singles out the bee: recurrent circuits are why being a bee may feel like something, a “tiny glow” beside a human “brighter light.”[35] “Consciousness and the Brain” and “Tracking the Footprints of Consciousness” return to the neural correlates of consciousness, Koch’s search with Francis Crick.[35] Rose reports a split between non-conscious “zombie” routines that keep the body alive and fleeting cortical ensembles offered as the basis of experience.[3] The zone stressed is posterior cerebral cortex, where elements are said to interact tightly.[35] The cerebellum, despite more neurons than the cortex, is said to lack the recurrent structure the account requires, so cell number is not treated as decisive.[35]

Wholes, measurement, and function

“Why We Need a Theory of Consciousness” pairs those features with physical postulates. Philip Goff notes that near page 75 the book calls the step an inference to the best explanation.[36] Anderson lists the principles as intrinsic existence, composition, information, integration, and exclusion.[35] Intrinsic existence is identified with Φ (phi), integrated causal power over a system’s own past and future, and with recurrent rather than purely feed-forward processing.[5] “Of Wholes” names a maximum of Φ a Whole and identifies its experience with a maximally irreducible cause–effect structure. Only that maximum counts as a subject, so crowds and the universe at large do not, and zero means the system does not exist for itself.[37] Rose’s illustration of exclusion is surgical separation of the corpus callosum: each hemisphere is described as its own Whole, whereas a rich link between two brains would replace both minds with one.[3] “Tools to Measure Consciousness” aims the same idea at the clinic. Magnetic pulses perturb the cortex of people regarded as being in a vegetative state, and the complexity of the echo is offered as a meter; Philip Ball quotes Koch’s image of the unconscious brain as a “stunted or cracked bell.”[35][5] “The Über-Mind and Pure Consciousness” distinguishes that blank from “pure consciousness,” a still-integrated but nearly silent cortex without sensation or memory.[35] Peter Kassan notes that Koch includes his time in a sensory-deprivation tank.[4] “Does Consciousness Have a Function?” uses simulated “animats” that navigate mazes and inherit successful wiring. Anderson reports the claim that the best integrators pack more distinctions about the world into their cause–effect structure, and that this advantage is why high-Φ systems would be retained.[35] Rose asks what function experience could then have for an atom.[3]

Computation, computers, and ubiquity

“Consciousness and Computationalism” and “Why Computers Can’t Experience” deny that experience is a program. Ball summarizes it as intrinsic causal architecture, not an input–output computation, so feed-forward digital machines are cast as zombies.[5] Rose adds that a simulated rainstorm is not wet, and that passing a Turing test would not confer experience; a brain model on a conventional computer is therefore said not to preserve a person.[3][35] Kassan records the caveat: a “neuromorphic” device unlike today’s computers might have human-level experience, while Koch still expects machines with human-level intelligence within decades.[4] “Is Consciousness Everywhere?” sets no switch-on point. Kassan reports suggestions that a bacterium, neurons in a dish, or an atom might have a mind; Koch marks the reach past animals, including single cells such as Paramecium, as speculation.[4][37] Rose reports that galaxies are not included.[3] The book sets the picture beside panpsychism, which Koch calls barren apart from IIT, though Kassan reads the theory itself as panpsychism renamed.[4] The coda, “Why This Matters,” returns to that contrast. Ball quotes the book’s description of a mind copied into circuitry as “fake consciousness—pretending by imitating people at the biophysical level.”[5]

Integrated information theory as presented

Axioms of experience

In The Feeling of Life Itself (MIT Press, 2019), Christof Koch presents integrated information theory, developed by Giulio Tononi, as an account of consciousness that starts from experience rather than from the brain.[38][23] The formulation is IIT 3.0, from Masafumi Oizumi, Larissa Albantakis, and Tononi in 2014, and from the 2016 review by Tononi, Melanie Boly, Marcello Massimini, and Koch: five properties said to hold of every experience are taken as axioms, and physical requirements are inferred from them.[38][23] Koch’s sentence, quoted by reviewers from the opening chapter, is that every conscious experience “exists for itself, is structured, is the specific way it is, is one, and is definite.”[36][4] Intrinsicality is the claim that an experience is real for the subject and does not depend on an outside observer, a point the 2016 review links to René Descartes’s insistence that one’s own experience is immediately evident.[23] Composition says an experience is structured, made of distinctions and their combinations, such as a color at a location.[38] Information, as an axiom rather than a communication measure, says each experience is specific: it is what it is by differing from alternatives.[38] Integration says it is unitary. Tononi’s illustration is that seeing the word “SONO” is not two half-experiences of “SO” and “NO.”[38] Exclusion says it is definite: one content, with borders and a grain in space and time, rather than less content or more.[38][23] Philip Ball calls these five “unalienable” properties of experience, including unity and definite content.[5] Koch does not leave the list as private introspection. Peter Kassan notes that he later calls the five features axioms and asserts that any system obeying them is conscious.[4] Philip Goff reports Koch’s claim that the step to the postulates is an inference to the best explanation, and that subjective feelings are “precisely described by these five axioms.”[36] The 2016 glossary calls the axioms “self-evident” and “true of every possible experience,” not laboratory findings; the 2014 paper begins with phenomenology and only then asks what mechanisms could account for it.[23][38] Kassan finds that distillation incomplete, and Goff argues that the quantitative translation cannot capture qualia.[4][36]

Postulates, Φ, and exclusion

The postulates are the proposed physical counterparts. To exist for itself, a substrate must have intrinsic cause–effect power: it must be possible to change it, and it must be able to make a difference to itself.[23] Ball quotes Koch’s line that this is “a system’s ability to be acted upon by its own state in the past and to influence its own future.”[5] Composition requires cause–effect power within the substrate, from single elements and from combinations.[23] The information postulate requires one specific cause–effect structure: repertoires by which mechanisms in their current state constrain past and future probabilities.[23][38] Integration requires that structure to be irreducible. The system measure is Φ (phi), the distance between the intact conceptual structure and the structure under its minimum information partition; φ is the same distance for one mechanism.[23][38] Exclusion picks out one substrate. The structure that, on the theory, “actually exists” is the maximally irreducible one, Φmax, at one set of elements and one spatio-temporal grain; overlapping candidates, and concepts short of φmax, are excluded.[23] IIT 3.0 calls a local maximum of integrated information a complex and identifies the experience with the conceptual structure that complex specifies, Φmax giving the proposed quantity and the structure’s form the quality.[38][23] The 2016 review treats this as an identity claim, notes that exhaustive calculation of Φ was not then feasible, and says the theory would be invalidated if the brain’s substrate of consciousness were not such a maximum.[23] Φ is not Claude Shannon’s quantity in information theory. A box in the 2016 review and a 2014 supplement separate them: Shannon information is what an outside observer can decode across a channel, and it requires neither integration nor exclusion.[23][39] IIT’s information is intrinsic, fixed by perturbing the system and asking which differences make a difference to it, with only maxima counted, so that the meaning is the conceptual structure rather than a message.[39] Steven Rose writes that Koch’s version is “different from and much richer than the concept of information used by AI and computational theorists.”[3] That contrast is a claim of the theory. Kassan cites Ronald Cicurel and Miguel Nicolelis, who argued that Φ still measures Shannon–Turing information.[4] The 2019 book states this 3.0 form. IIT 4.0, published by Albantakis, Tononi, and colleagues in 2023, later separates a “zeroth” axiom of bare existence from intrinsicality and unfolds distinctions and relations with a revised measure.[40]

Brains and clinical measurement

In The Feeling of Life Itself (MIT Press, 2019), Christof Koch uses integrated information theory to place consciousness in particular cortical circuitry and to describe a way of probing it without behavior.[3][5] The anatomy and the bedside test restate a 2016 review with Marcello Massimini, Mélanie Boly and Giulio Tononi, plus earlier stimulation–EEG experiments.[41][42] The book is more definite than those papers about where experience sits and what the clinical number means.

Posterior cortex and the cerebellum

Steven Rose, reviewing the book, summarized Koch’s claim that fleeting neuronal ensembles in one region of the cerebral cortex form the substrate of experience, a “Whole.”[3] Philip Ball reported Koch’s suspicion that the seat of consciousness is a posterior cortical region with recurrent feedback, not a feed-forward net.[5] A 2018 essay stating the same picture names a “posterior hot zone” in parietal, occipital and temporal cortex as the neural correlates of consciousness for sights, sounds and feelings.[43] Koch writes that stimulation there can yield flashes, shapes, distorted faces, hallucinations or an urge to move, while frontal stimulation generally yields none, and that small posterior lesions can erase content such as faces, motion, color or space.[43] Large historical removals of prefrontal cortex, he says, left patients conscious even when inhibition and planning suffered.[43] The 2016 review, using the phrase “posterior cortical hot zone,” is more guarded: once report, task and arousal are set aside, posterior cortex, including sensory areas, is the best candidate, not a frontoparietal network for monitoring and reporting—though some prefrontal neurons might still carry contents such as affect.[41] The cerebellum is the contrast. Koch states that it holds about 69 billion neurons—mostly granule cells, about four times the rest of the brain—plus Purkinje cells, yet cerebellar loss or congenital absence is not described as abolishing sight, hearing, feeling or a sense of self.[43] He attributes that to a largely feed-forward, modular layout, which the theory treats as low in integrated information.[43] The 2016 review records the clinical dissociation, including a case of complete cerebellar agenesis with mostly preserved cognition, and notes that cerebellar activity can covary with perception without constituting it.[41] It does not calculate integrated information for those circuits.[41]

The perturbational complexity index

The instrument the book describes comes from the same program. Ball recounts Koch’s image of magnetic pulses in the cortex: a conscious brain rings like a tuned bell, while an unconscious one “acts like a stunted or cracked bell.”[5] The measure is the perturbational complexity index (PCI), introduced in 2013 by Adenauer G. Casali, Massimini, Tononi and colleagues.[42] Transcranial magnetic stimulation perturbs cortex; high-density electroencephalography records the response; Lempel–Ziv compression of the significant sources, normalized by source entropy, yields a number from 0 to 1.[42] That follows the claim that consciousness needs both integration and differentiation. Casali and colleagues note that indices of integrated information itself had been tried only on small simulations, not on human brains, so PCI is a proxy for the evoked balance, not a value of Φ.[42] In the 2013 data, PCI separated wakefulness and dreaming from non-REM sleep and from midazolam, xenon and propofol anesthesia in single subjects, and sorted a smaller group in a vegetative state, a minimally conscious state or locked-in syndrome.[42] A 2016 study fixed PCI* = 0.31 on 150 people who could report experience—100% sensitivity and specificity there—then applied it to 81 patients with disorders of consciousness.[44] Thirty-six of 38 minimally conscious patients exceeded it, as did 9 of 43 diagnosed as vegetative, which the authors read as possible consciousness without behavioral expression.[44] Koch’s 2018 “zap-and-zip” retelling, the version nearest the book, repeats those counts.[43] The papers claim that this evoked complexity tracks reported level of consciousness, not that a high PCI is a measured Φ.[42][41]

Animals and the extent of experience

Nonhuman animals

In The Feeling of Life Itself, Christof Koch treats consciousness as experience and argues that it is widespread among nonhuman animals: from behavioral, physiological, and developmental similarities, mammals are said to have sights, sounds, pains, and pleasures, though not necessarily as richly as people; birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish are included on related forebrain structure; and invertebrates that sense and learn are included as well, among them octopuses and honey bees, cited for face recognition, the waggle dance, maze memory, and a small but densely recurrent brain; following Charles Darwin on earthworms, he writes that no Rubicon separates sentient from nonsentient animals, so experience is supposed to grow poorer and slower as nervous systems simplify rather than stop at a line, while evidence about plants is left preliminary because such behavior need not be felt.[45] That is wider than the Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness of 7 July 2012, which Koch helped edit and proclaimed with Philip Low and David Edelman.[46] The declaration stated that lack of a neocortex does not appear to preclude affective states, and that mammals, birds, and many other creatures, including octopuses, have neurological substrates of conscious states, while noting that animals often cannot report them. The groups named are mammals, birds, and other creatures including octopuses, not microbes.[46] Philip Ball describes Koch’s view as making consciousness a matter of degree—“a fundamental, elementary property of living matter”—significant in human brains and other animals, but not spread equally, and separable from intelligence.[5] Steven Rose answered that most biologists instead treat consciousness as an evolved property of animals with sufficiently complex brains.[3] Ball adds that integrated information theory, taken here from Giulio Tononi, is one proposal among others, with no resolution among neuroscientists or philosophers of mind.[5]

How far consciousness is said to extend

The book then drops any threshold at which experience would switch on: whatever has a maximum of integrated information, Φ, above zero is said to exist for itself to that degree, so Koch suggests a “tiny glow” for single cells such as Paramecium and Escherichia coli, treats an atom’s “this-rather-than-not-this” as speculation, and says experience grows richer from a fetus to a teenager to an adult cortex; exclusion, counting only maxima of Φ, then withholds an inner life from hives, crowds, spoons, chairs, dunes, and the universe at large even if some constituents are allowed a minimal one.[45] Peter Kassan reports the same steps in plainer terms—a bacterium or a single atom may be ascribed a mind, neurons grown in a dish may form a “mini-mind,” and “brute matter” is not excluded—and notes that Koch calls panpsychism, apart from this theory, barren.[4] Kassan argues that the extension conflicts with the evolutionary maxim the book quotes, that a bacterium cannot confirm any detector, and that even three linked elements with nonzero Φ would count as minded.[4] Rose records the boundary as atoms perhaps included and galaxies not, and asks what function consciousness could serve for an atom.[3] The ethical questions are not closed by the measure. If feeling tracks intrinsic causal structure rather than intelligent performance, some animals and much simpler systems could be candidates for consideration while a clever simulation would not.[5] A 2023 letter from 124 researchers, which Nature reported as an uproar over labeling the theory pseudoscience, warned that taking it as established would bear on animal and organoid testing, stem-cell research, and abortion: the signatories said it treats dish-grown organoids and very early human fetuses as likely conscious, claims they regarded as untested.[47][48]

Computers and causal powers

Simulation is not instantiation

The Feeling of Life Itself (MIT Press, 2019) treats the inability of conventional digital computers to have experience as a consequence of integrated information theory. Christof Koch, chief scientist and president of the Allen Institute for Brain Science, presents the theory developed with Giulio Tononi: consciousness is a system’s intrinsic causal power—how far its present state constrains its own past and future—not the functions an outside observer sees it perform. [49] [5] In a December 2019 Scientific American essay that lists the book among its sources, Koch writes that programmable computers have only minute integrated information, Φ, and that Φ does not depend on whether the software calculates taxes or simulates a brain. [49] Two networks can share an input–output map and still differ in Φ because of their wiring, so that one has experience and a behavioral duplicate has none. [49] Philip Ball’s review reports the architectural version: merely feeding information forward, as digital computers do, yields only what Koch calls zombie intelligence, whereas experience, on this account, requires the feedback Koch locates in posterior cortex. [5] The operative distinction is between simulating a process and instantiating it. Steven Rose, in Times Higher Education, gives Koch’s analogy: a computer may simulate a rainstorm without being wet inside, and a machine as complex as a brain would remain a zombie that might pass a Turing test without having an experience. [3] Koch applies the point to a scanned connectome run as software and to mind uploading: if only function mattered, the simulation would continue the person; under the theory it would act like that person while feeling nothing, “the ultimate deepfake.” [49] Ball reports the book’s matching verdict—“nothing but clever programming,” “fake consciousness” that imitates people at the biophysical level. [5]

Functionalism and artificial intelligence

The claim is set against functionalist and computational accounts, which remain live alternatives. Ball quotes Koch’s rejection of what he calls the age’s dominant myth: that “consciousness is just a smart hack away,” and that people are “Turing machines made flesh, robots unaware of our own programming.” [5] Koch contrasts the theory with global neuronal workspace theory, associated with Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux, on which consciousness is global broadcast and a faithful simulation could be conscious. [49] He denies that inference—“consciousness is about being, not about doing”—while Ball notes that the dispute is unsettled. [49] [5] The book does not say that no artifact could feel. Peter Kassan’s Skeptical Inquirer review describes a chapter, “Why Computers Can’t Experience,” in which Koch contrasts universal Turing machines, and quantum computers as he discusses them, with a “neuromorphic electronic device” that “could have human-level experience.” [4] The essay likewise requires neuromorphic hardware built like a nervous system, rather than a program on a conventional processor. [49] Kassan replies that a device is a computer only from an observer’s point of view, and that the exception blurs the subtitle’s anti-computational claim. [4] Intelligence is separated from experience. Ball reports that Koch allows consciousness with little intelligence, and intelligence with none, so success at games or at an imitation test would not show an inner life. [5] Kassan notes the forecast of human-level machine intelligence within decades—the “birth of true artificial intelligence”—without experience, and that Koch confines John Searle’s Chinese room argument—that manipulating symbols need not be understanding—to a footnote attacking Searle for turning it on the theory. [4] Philip Goff’s review cites Scott Aaronson’s 2014 criticisms for the theory’s “absurd implications.” [36] Aaronson argues that large Φ arises in error-correcting codes and in logic gates wired as expander graphs, which he does not take to be conscious: “The brain might be an expander, but not every expander is a brain.” [50]

Philosophical setting

The hard problem and illusionism

The Feeling of Life Itself starts from experience rather than from neural function. The hard problem of consciousness, as David Chalmers framed it, is why physical processing should feel like anything. Matthew Owen notes that Christof Koch uses that problem to motivate integrated information theory: consciousness is taken as fundamental, and the physical substrate is inferred from experience.[51] Koch’s formulation is that the theory “does not bang its head” against the hard problem by trying to “squeeze the juice of consciousness out of the brain.”[51] Talis Bachmann describes the same move, from how experience feels to how matter must be organized.[52] Koch treats the reality of experience as non-negotiable. He calls the eliminative materialism of Patricia Churchland and Paul Churchland the “metaphysical counterpart to Cotard’s syndrome,” and Daniel Dennett’s claim that consciousness is an illusion “absurd.”[51] Quoting John Searle, he offers both as evidence that much twentieth-century analytic philosophy had “gone to the dogs,” and he appeals to René Descartes’s cogito.[51] Philip Goff argues that this does not close the gap: the postulates capture structure, not qualities such as blue, and Koch’s comparison of “Why does integrated information feel like anything?” to questions about physical law leaves the hard problem standing.[36] The 2013 exchange with Searle, over Koch’s previous book, was only partly an alliance. In The New York Review of Books, Searle argued that information is observer-relative.[53] He quoted Giulio Tononi’s claim of “exactly 1 bit of consciousness” for a photodiode, called the result a reductio, and read Koch as committed to panpsychism about protons and ordinary devices.[53] Koch and Tononi answered that their information is intrinsic—“differences that make a difference” to the system—and that only local maxima are conscious.[53] Searle replied that the information was still observer-relative and that nothing explained why the structure should feel like anything.[53] Kassan reports that, in the 2019 book, the Chinese room argument is confined to a footnote objecting to its use against the theory, after meetings that had not persuaded Searle.[4]

Rival scientific theories

The empirical rival Koch draws most sharply is global workspace theory, from Bernard Baars and, neurally, Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux. In Scientific American for December 2019, which lists the book for further reading, he describes a limited shared “blackboard”: information is conscious when broadcast to language, planning, memory, and action.[54] Crossing a threshold ignites long-range neurons in prefrontal, parietotemporal, and cingulate cortex; consciousness, he writes, is then “just a clever hack away,” so sophisticated computers would be conscious.[54] Integrated information theory, as he contrasts it, identifies experience with intrinsic causal power, Φ. Systems with the same input–output behavior can differ in Φ, so a simulation can act like a person and feel nothing, as a weather model is not wet.[54] The split, on his summary, is function—in the manner of functionalism—versus intrinsic causal power, being rather than doing, and a disagreement over a frontal or posterior epicenter.[54] Bachmann finds that anatomy in the book: a posterior “hot zone” for sensory experience, with prefrontal cortex for intelligence and control rather than for the correlate itself, against Dehaene and Changeux and against higher-order theories of consciousness.[52] Paul Thagard instead prefers Dehaene’s account, Michael Graziano’s attention schema theory, and his own semantic-pointer account, rejecting both slight consciousness for simple feedback devices and the book’s denial of it to existing computers.[55] Steven Rose contrasts a reach that may include atoms, though not galaxies, with the biological claim that consciousness evolved only with complex nervous systems.[3]

Reception

Favorable assessments

In New Scientist on 30 October 2019, Alun Anderson set The Feeling of Life Itself apart from a crowded field of speculative writing on consciousness. He called Christof Koch’s “deep synthesis of a profound theory of consciousness and the latest neuroscience” an exception, and continued, “At last, we have a theory.”[56] Philip Ball’s 25 January 2020 essay in Prospect (March 2020 issue) presented Koch’s rejection of the idea that, in artificial intelligence, “consciousness is just a smart hack away.”[5] Reporting integrated information theory as developed by Giulio Tononi, Ball wrote that machines built like present computers never become conscious, and that a brain-like device would be “like a golem stumbling about”—possibly able to pass a Turing test, still a zombie.[5] He noted the magnetic-pulse test in which, Koch says, an unconscious brain “acts like a stunted or cracked bell,” and the claim that uploading a mind would yield only “fake consciousness,” while adding that the view was one among many and still unresolved.[5] In Consciousness and Cognition (2020), Talis Bachmann of the University of Tartu called the book’s account, shaped with Francis Crick and later with Tononi, “one of the most elaborated” then available.[57] It “deserves serious attention,” he wrote, for its empirical support, its start from first-person axioms rather than computation, and its posterior “hot zone” set against prefrontal theories.[57] Philip Goff, in the American Journal of Psychology (vol. 133, no. 4, 2020, pp. 523–526), called the book “a deeply enjoyable journey” and a “wonderfully accessible introduction,” and the theory “a pioneering innovation which yields many important insights,” one that might still inform later panpsychist theories.[36] Matthew Owen, in Philosophia Christi (vol. 22, no. 1, 2020, pp. 145–156), wrote that making those philosophical commitments explicit “ought to be applauded by even the most ardent philosophical opponent,” and that the book was “essential reading” on the neurobiology of consciousness and one of its leading theories.[58][51]

Objections in reviews

Steven Rose, emeritus professor of neuroscience at the Open University, was “unconvinced” in Times Higher Education (19 September 2019).[3] He summarized consciousness as a “freeze-frame” of experience in a cortical Whole, set against nonconscious “zombie” routines: a split brain would have two Wholes, linked brains one identity, and a computer could pass a Turing test yet remain a zombie, since a simulated rainstorm is not wet.[3] He would not follow the claim that consciousness “pervades the universe,” perhaps even atoms though not galaxies, and asked what function it could have for an atom if, as most biologists hold, experience evolved in animals with complex brains.[3] Peter Kassan, in Skeptical Inquirer (May/June 2020), called the book a bad marriage of a scientific half and a “wildly speculative” half, the first used to lend credibility to the second.[4] He accepted the anatomy, the correlate research, and the clinical problem of unresponsive patients, and much of the critique of computationalism, but not the promotion of five features of Koch’s own experience—“exists for itself, is structured, is the specific way it is, is one, and is definite”—into axioms necessary and sufficient for consciousness.[4] On that standard the book’s three-element diagram would give a mind to three transistors, and Koch’s further suggestions a mind to a bacterium, a cell, or “perhaps even brute matter itself.”[4] Even a correct measure of integrated information, he wrote, would not explain qualitative experience, and Koch’s charge that computationalism is “ideology run amok” fit the theory itself, which Kassan likened to Frank Tipler’s The Physics of Immortality.[4] Goff’s “friendly challenge” was that the postulates are quantitative and causal, whereas experience “essentially involves qualities”—the blueness of blue, the feel of cold—that this vocabulary cannot capture.[36] Identifying a maximum of integrated information with those qualities would, he argued, drop the introspective justification and leave the hard problem of consciousness unmet, so the theory “doesn’t tell the full story.”[36] Owen argued that experience cannot be strictly identical with the substrate’s causal structure, since a subject knows the experience directly and those neural interactions only indirectly, and that widespread cellular consciousness does not itself yield Koch’s call to drop anthropocentrism or to prefer vegetarianism: the theory states no account of value, and a consciousness-based “moral ladder” leaves out nonconscious things such as ecosystems.[51] Bachmann doubted contentless “pure” experience, meditation included, because emptiness is still a feeling; he also found a purely cortical hot zone, leaving out brainstem and thalamus, poorly supported, and Koch’s answer to why integrated information should feel like anything unconvincing.[57]

Later developments

Tests, the wager, and the pseudoscience dispute

On 23 June 2023, at the annual meeting of the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness at New York University, Christof Koch and David Chalmers settled a 1998 wager. Koch had bet a case of wine that a clear neural pattern of consciousness would be known within 25 years; both agreed it was not, and Chalmers won.[6][7] Koch then bet again, to 2048; Chalmers accepted, hoping to lose but suspecting he would win.[7] The meeting also heard an adversarial test of integrated information theory (IIT), the framework of The Feeling of Life Itself, against global neuronal workspace theory, reported at the time as inconclusive.[7] Published in Nature on 30 April 2025, the study used functional MRI, magnetoencephalography, and intracranial EEG in 256 people. Content was found in visual, ventrotemporal, and inferior frontal cortex, with sustained posterior activity and content-specific frontal–visual synchronization. Some predictions of each theory held, but both were challenged: IIT by missing sustained synchronization within posterior cortex, and global neuronal workspace theory by absent offset ignition and limited prefrontal detail.[59] Sustained posterior activity fits the 2019 book’s siting of experience there; the missing synchrony undercuts the claim that integration in that network specifies it. On 15 September 2023, 124 scholars posted a PsyArXiv letter, “The Integrated Information Theory of Consciousness as Pseudoscience.” Drafters included Stephen M. Fleming and Hakwan Lau; signatories included Daniel Dennett. They wrote that Nature and Science had presented IIT as a leading, tested theory on predictions not tied to its core, and that journalists were briefed before a preprint. Citing panpsychist commitments, they said IIT can treat an inactive grid of logic gates as conscious, possibly more so than a human, and organoids, very early fetuses, and on some interpretations plants, as conscious as well. Until the whole theory was testable, they said the label should apply, given stakes from coma care to abortion, while denying that IIT lacked intellectual merit.[60] The imaging study did not test that extension of experience beyond useful computation, which the 2019 book defends. Replies divided over the label, not over whether IIT might be false. Koch, of the Allen Institute for Brain Science, said it “may be empirically wrong” but states its assumptions explicitly. Anil Seth called the label inflammatory: ambition makes the core harder to test, though some neural predictions can be tested. The report noted a 2022 review of 101 empirical IIT studies. Liad Mudrik, a co-leader of the adversarial study, said it had falsified one IIT prediction and that denunciation could feed skepticism of the field. Erik Hoel, a former student of Giulio Tononi, said imperfect measurements constrain every theory and that IIT had been singled out. Lau glossed the label as a poorly supported claim presented as established.[8]

Revisions of the theory and later writing

In October 2023, Larissa Albantakis, Tononi, and colleagues published IIT 4.0 in PLOS Computational Biology: axioms of experience, postulates for a substrate, a unique measure of intrinsic information consistent with the postulates, and explicit causal relations. Quality of experience is the distinctions and relations unfolded from irreducible cause–effect power, in principle for any system of units in a state.[40] The 2019 book’s formalism is revised; the identity of experience with that structure is not. Then I Am Myself the World (Basic Books, 2024) repeats that identity. A Science News review reports that Koch rejects consciousness-as-software and the “Turing machine made flesh”: against functionalism, performing a function is not instantiating experience. He said a dog without weekend plans still feels pain and joy. The review reports his claim that ChatGPT has an “itsy, bitsy, little bit” of consciousness, less than a worm with about 300 neurons, and that a simulated brain is not conscious, as a simulated black hole does not attract, because transistors link to few partners while a neuron can contact thousands.[61]
