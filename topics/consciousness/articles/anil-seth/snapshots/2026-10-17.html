Anil Seth

Fact-checked by Grok · 9 months ago
Anil Seth is a British neuroscientist specializing in cognitive and computational neuroscience, particularly the scientific study of consciousness, and serves as Professor of Cognitive and Computational Neuroscience at the University of Sussex, where he also directs the Sussex Centre for Consciousness Science.[1][2] Born in 1972 in Oxford, England, Seth earned a B.A. in Natural Sciences from the University of Cambridge in 1994, followed by an M.Sc. in Knowledge-Based Systems from the University of Sussex in 1996, and a D.Phil. in Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence from the same institution in 2001.[3] After completing his doctorate, he held a postdoctoral fellowship and associate fellowship at The Neurosciences Institute in San Diego from 2001 to 2006, before joining the University of Sussex as permanent faculty in 2006.[1][3] He advanced to Reader (Associate Professor) in the School of Informatics and became Director of the Sussex Centre for Consciousness Science in 2010, later assuming his current professorship in 2012.[1][3] Seth's research focuses on the brain mechanisms underlying consciousness, perception, and selfhood, employing predictive processing frameworks and neuroimaging techniques to explore how the brain generates subjective experience.[1][2] He co-directs the CIFAR Program on Brain, Mind, and Consciousness and leads the Dreamachine project, an initiative using stroboscopic light to induce shared visual experiences for consciousness research.[1][2] As Founding Editor-in-Chief of the journal Neuroscience of Consciousness from 2014 to 2024, he has shaped the field's discourse.[2] Among his notable contributions, Seth authored the bestselling book Being You: A New Science of Consciousness (2021), which synthesizes decades of research on the predictive nature of perception and consciousness, and co-edited 30-Second Brain (2014), a popular science overview of neuroscience topics.[2] His 2017 TED talk, "How your brain hallucinates your conscious reality," has garnered over 15 million views, highlighting his ability to communicate complex ideas to broad audiences.[2] Seth's work has been recognized with the Clarivate Highly Cited Researcher designation since 2019, the Royal Society Michael Faraday Prize in 2023 for advancing public understanding of science, and the Humanists UK Rationalist Press Medal in 2025.[2] His research has been funded by prestigious bodies including the EPSRC, ERC, Wellcome Trust, and CIFAR, underscoring its impact on neuroscience and philosophy of mind.[2]

Early life and education

Early life

Anil Seth was born on 11 June 1972 in Oxford, England. He spent his early childhood in Letcombe Regis, a small rural village in Oxfordshire, where the family had settled after his father's arrival in Britain in 1947.[4][5][6] Seth's father, Bhola Seth, was born in Allahabad, India, in 1927 and immigrated to the United Kingdom at age 20 to pursue higher education; he earned a BSc from Allahabad University in 1945, followed by a PhD in mechanical engineering from the University of Sheffield, and worked as a research scientist at the Esso Research Centre near Abingdon from 1961 until his retirement in 1992. Bhola was also an accomplished athlete, winning the veterans' world doubles badminton title in 1976.[5] Seth's mother, Ann Seth (née Delaney), was born in York in 1934 and grew up amid the hardships of wartime Britain; she began her career as a teacher of English and history before transitioning to visual arts, earning a degree in visual studies and creating multimedia installations that documented the Oxfordshire countryside.[7] The couple married in 1960 and had one son, providing a supportive household blending Indian heritage with British rural life.[5][7] From a young age, Seth exhibited a profound curiosity about the mind and subjective experience, questioning phenomena such as "Why am I me and not someone else?" and "Where was I before I was born?". This early fascination with consciousness and the brain was nurtured in the expansive rural setting of Oxfordshire, where he enjoyed an unrestricted childhood exploring the countryside, honing observational skills that would influence his lifelong interest in science and perception.[8][6]

Education

Anil Seth earned a Bachelor of Arts in Natural Sciences from King's College, University of Cambridge, in 1994, which was subsequently promoted to a Master of Arts per university tradition. His undergraduate studies initially emphasized physics before shifting to experimental psychology, laying foundational knowledge in neuroscience and cognitive processes.[3][9][10] He continued his graduate education at the University of Sussex, obtaining a Master of Science in Knowledge-Based Systems with distinction in 1996. Seth then completed a Doctor of Philosophy in Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence in 2001, with his doctoral thesis titled On the Relations between Behaviour, Mechanism, and Environment, which examined agent-environment interactions through computational modeling. This work introduced him to key concepts in cognitive computation and adaptive systems.[3][1][11] Following his PhD, Seth conducted postdoctoral research as a Fellow and Associate Fellow at The Neurosciences Institute in San Diego from 2001 to 2006. There, he collaborated with Nobel laureate Gerald Edelman on computational models of brain function, gaining significant influences from Edelman's theoretical neuroscience framework, particularly regarding dynamic neural processes and consciousness.[1][9]

Professional career

Academic positions

Anil Seth completed his postdoctoral research at The Neurosciences Institute in San Diego, California, from 2001 to 2006, before returning to the United Kingdom.[3] In 2006, he rejoined the University of Sussex as a lecturer in the Department of Informatics, focusing on cognitive neuroscience within the School of Cognitive and Computing Sciences (later restructured as part of the School of Engineering and Informatics).[12] This relocation from the United States to Sussex marked a significant transition, enabling Seth to integrate his computational expertise with the university's interdisciplinary environment in psychology and informatics, which broadened his contributions to neuroscience education and faculty collaborations.[13] Seth progressed through the academic ranks at Sussex, serving as senior lecturer and then reader from approximately 2006 to 2012, during which he contributed to departmental teaching in computational neuroscience and seminars in neurosciences.[13] He was promoted to full professor of Cognitive and Computational Neuroscience in 2012, a position he continues to hold.[1] In this role, Seth has undertaken administrative duties within the School of Engineering and Informatics, including coordinating advanced courses on adaptive behavior and theoretical neuroscience.[10] In addition to his primary appointment, Seth has held visiting positions, notably as Visiting Professor in the Department of Psychology at the University of Amsterdam since 2011, facilitating international collaborations in cognitive science.[10] These roles have supported his ongoing involvement in European academic networks without interrupting his core faculty responsibilities at Sussex.

Leadership roles

Anil Seth co-founded the Sackler Centre for Consciousness Science at the University of Sussex in 2010, serving as co-director alongside Hugo Critchley, with initial funding from the Dr. Mortimer and Theresa Sackler Foundation.[14][15][16] The centre, later rebranded as the Sussex Centre for Consciousness Science, focuses on advancing the scientific understanding of consciousness through interdisciplinary research in neuroscience, philosophy, and computational modeling.[17] Seth has continued in a leadership capacity, becoming the sole director by the mid-2010s and maintaining this role as of 2025.[1] In 2019, Seth was appointed co-director of the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research (CIFAR) Azrieli Program in Brain, Mind & Consciousness, collaborating with György Buzsáki to foster global research on the neural and cognitive foundations of awareness.[18][19] This program emphasizes international collaborations, integrating perspectives from biology, physics, and artificial intelligence to explore consciousness across species and systems.[20] Additionally, Seth served as president of the British Science Association's Psychology Section in 2017, overseeing events and initiatives to promote psychological sciences to a broader audience.[21] Seth founded and edited Neuroscience of Consciousness, an open-access journal published by Oxford University Press, from 2014 to 2024, during which he co-edited with Jakob Hohwy and elevated the publication as a key venue for consciousness studies.[2][22] In 2021, he assumed leadership of the European Research Council (ERC) Advanced Grant project CONSCIOUS, a five-year initiative funded with €2.43 million to investigate the neural mechanisms of conscious perception using predictive coding frameworks.[23][24] As of 2025, this project remains active under his principal investigatorship, contributing to ongoing international efforts at the intersection of consciousness science and artificial intelligence ethics.[25]

Research contributions

Consciousness science

Anil Seth has developed a influential perspective on consciousness, framing it as a form of controlled hallucination in which the brain generates subjective experiences by predicting and constructing sensory realities rather than merely receiving passive inputs. This theory posits that perceptions are actively shaped by the brain's internal models, constrained by sensory evidence to align with the external world, thereby enabling adaptive behavior and self-awareness. Seth articulates this view in detail through his synthesis of neuroscientific evidence, emphasizing how such generative processes underpin the vividness and personal nature of conscious experience. To empirically probe these ideas, Seth's research employs advanced neuroimaging techniques, including functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG), to examine disorders of consciousness such as vegetative states and anesthesia-induced unconsciousness. These methods allow detection of residual awareness through patterns of brain activity, like task-based decoding of imagined movements or perturbations via transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), revealing that up to 15-20% of clinically diagnosed unresponsive patients may retain covert consciousness. Such experiments not only refine diagnostic tools but also illuminate the neural boundaries of conscious states, showing how disruptions in predictive mechanisms contribute to loss of awareness.[26] Seth's contributions extend to integrating first-person phenomenological descriptions—such as subjective reports of qualia and presence—with third-person neuroscience data, fostering a naturalistic approach to the hard problem of consciousness. Rather than treating subjective experience as an inexplicable add-on to physical processes, he advocates building explanatory bridges that account for why specific brain dynamics give rise to felt experiences, drawing on embodied and predictive frameworks to demystify qualia without resorting to dualism. This integrative method prioritizes testable hypotheses over philosophical standoffs, advancing a science of consciousness grounded in biological realism. Central to Seth's framework are concepts like phenomenological control, the capacity of the brain to modulate conscious content in accordance with prior expectancies and beliefs, as evidenced in studies of hypnotic suggestion and perceptual illusions where participants generate experiences on demand. Complementing this, he emphasizes interoception's role in selfhood, arguing that the minimal sense of self arises from predictive inferences about internal bodily signals, such as heartbeats and visceral sensations, which anchor the "I" in physiological homeostasis and emotional valence. These ideas highlight how consciousness is deeply embodied, emerging from the brain's ongoing regulation of the living body. By 2025, Seth has authored over 340 peer-reviewed publications, including seminal works on the emergence of consciousness in complex neural systems through measures of integration and differentiation, which underscore how global brain states differentiate conscious from unconscious processing. A milestone in this trajectory is his 2025 co-authored article in Frontiers in Science, which provides an overview of recent developments in consciousness science and proposes future interdisciplinary paradigms for advancing the field, such as combined EEG/fMRI protocols and computational modeling. This body of work positions consciousness as an adaptive, brain-bound phenomenon, informed briefly by predictive processing as a computational substrate for hallucination-like generation.[27][28]

Predictive processing and related theories

Anil Seth has advanced the predictive processing framework, positing the brain as a hierarchical prediction machine that generates top-down expectations about sensory inputs to minimize prediction errors, thereby enabling efficient perception and action. In this view, perception arises not as passive reception of sensory data but as active inference, where the brain continuously updates its generative models to reduce discrepancies between predicted and actual sensory signals. Seth's adaptations emphasize sensorimotor contingencies, integrating predictions with bodily actions to explain perceptual presence, such as why certain stimuli feel vividly "present" while others do not. This approach draws on Bayesian principles, where perceptions are probabilistic inferences optimizing for accuracy and simplicity.[29][30] A cornerstone of Seth's modeling involves Granger causality to dissect directed interactions in neural dynamics, providing a statistical tool to infer causal influences from time-series data in brain networks. By applying Granger causality, Seth and collaborators have characterized how neural populations exhibit emergent connectivity patterns during behavior, distinguishing effective connectivity from mere correlations. This method, extended through graph-theoretic analyses, reveals causal cores—subsets of interactions critical for behavioral outputs—in simulated and empirical neural systems. For instance, frequency-domain decompositions of conditional Granger causality allow mapping of dynamic causal flows, aiding the study of complexity in brain-wide networks.[31][32][33] Seth's theoretical contributions incorporate Bayesian inference equations tailored to perceptual processes, often framed within the free-energy principle. Central to this is the minimization of surprise, quantified as the self-information or negative log-probability of sensory inputs:
S=−log⁡P(x) S = -\log P(x) S=−logP(x)
 where $ S $ represents surprise, $ P(x) $ is the probability of sensory data $ x $ under the brain's model, and the system seeks to bound this via variational free energy $ F $, an upper bound on surprise that approximates Bayesian posteriors through gradient descent. In Seth's applications, this formulation underpins active inference, where actions are selected to fulfill predictions and resolve uncertainties, as seen in models of sensorimotor learning. These derivations highlight how hierarchical predictive coding resolves perceptual ambiguities by propagating errors bidirectionally across neural layers.[30][34] Seth extends predictive processing to artificial intelligence, arguing in a 2025 target article that conscious AI requires more than computational equivalence to biological systems, advocating biological naturalism to emphasize embodied, homeostatic processes over disembodied algorithms. This challenges substrate-independent views of consciousness, positing that AI's lack of biological grounding limits its capacity for genuine subjective experience, while urging integration of predictive principles into AI architectures for safer, more interpretable systems. His analyses of causality and emergence in brain networks further inform these extensions, modeling complexity as arising from adaptive inference in open, self-organizing systems.[35] Empirically, Seth's Dreamachine project investigates induced visual hallucinations through stroboscopic flicker, using predictive processing to interpret how sensory perturbations disrupt error-minimizing predictions, yielding insights into hallucinatory mechanisms. Complementing this, the ERC-funded CONSCIOUS project (2021–2026) employs advanced neuroimaging and computational modeling to mechanistically unpack conscious experiences, testing predictive frameworks against neural data from altered states. These initiatives operationalize theoretical predictions, linking abstract models to measurable brain dynamics.[24][36][23]

Publications

Scientific papers

Anil Seth has authored or co-authored over 340 peer-reviewed scientific papers as of 2025.[27] His work has garnered more than 38,900 citations, with an h-index of 92 and an i10-index of 237, reflecting substantial influence in the field.[37] Seth has been recognized as a Clarivate Highly Cited Researcher from 2019 to 2025, placing him in the top 0.1% of researchers globally based on citation impact.[2][38] His publications span key themes in neuroscience of consciousness, cognitive science, and intersections with artificial intelligence, appearing in prominent journals such as Nature Reviews Neuroscience, Trends in Cognitive Sciences, and Behavioral and Brain Sciences.[39] Representative works explore predictive processing frameworks, embodied cognition, and the neural basis of subjective experience, often integrating computational models with empirical data.[40] In 2025, beyond the target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, Seth co-authored additional papers, including one in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (September 2025) on predictive models in neuroscience.[41] Notable papers include Seth's 2025 target article "Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism" in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, which examines consciousness in AI systems through a naturalistic lens.[39] Earlier seminal contributions encompass "Theories of consciousness" (2022, Nature Reviews Neuroscience, co-authored with Tim Bayne), providing a comprehensive survey of leading theories in the field,[39] and "Interoceptive inference, emotion, and the embodied self" (2013, Trends in Cognitive Sciences), which has received over 700 citations and links predictive processing to emotional and self-related experiences.[42] Another high-impact paper, "Predictive processing as a systematic basis for identifying the neural correlates of consciousness" (2020, Philosophy and the Mind Sciences, co-authored with Jakob Hohwy), has advanced methodological approaches in consciousness research and amassed over 100 citations by 2025.[43] Seth has collaborated extensively with Karl Friston on applications of the free-energy principle, including the 2016 paper "Active interoceptive inference and the emotional brain" in Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, which integrates hierarchical inference models with emotional processing and has been cited over 500 times.[44] These joint efforts underscore Seth's role in bridging theoretical neuroscience and predictive coding paradigms.[45]

Books

Anil Seth's most prominent book, Being You: A New Science of Consciousness, published in 2021 by Faber & Faber in the UK and Dutton in the US, presents a comprehensive exploration of consciousness as a biological phenomenon shaped by the brain's predictive processes.[46] In it, Seth argues that conscious experience arises from the brain's generation of "controlled hallucinations," where perceptions are actively constructed to model the world and maintain a sense of self, drawing on neuroscience, philosophy, and personal anecdotes to challenge traditional views of reality and identity.[46] The book became a Sunday Times Top 10 Bestseller, Guardian Book of the Week, and was named a Best Book of 2021 by outlets including the New Statesman, Economist, Bloomberg Businessweek, and Financial Times; it has been translated into languages such as Spanish, Italian, French, Korean, Japanese, Finnish, Russian, and Romanian, with the Russian edition winning the 2023 Zimin Foundation Enlightener Translation Prize.[47][48] Earlier in his career, Seth served as editor for 30-Second Brain: The 50 Key Ideas, Concepts, and Breakthroughs in Neuroscience, Each Explained in Half a Minute, published in 2014 by Ivy Press, which distills complex neuroscience topics—from brain chemistry and neural networks to cognitive biases—into concise, accessible summaries aimed at general readers.[47] As consultant for Eye Benders: The Science of Seeing and Believing (Ivy Press, 2013), co-authored with Clive Gifford, Seth contributed scientific insights to a children's book featuring optical illusions that illustrate how the brain interprets visual information, blending puzzles with explanations of perceptual neuroscience.[47][49] The work won the 2014 Royal Society Young People's Book Prize for its engaging introduction to vision science.[49] Seth also consulted on Brain Twisters (Ivy Press, 2015), co-authored with Clive Gifford, a follow-up to Eye Benders that targets young readers with brain games, mind tricks, and puzzles to demonstrate principles of neuroscience, such as attention, memory, and decision-making.[47] These earlier publications reflect Seth's commitment to science communication for non-specialist audiences, particularly youth, by using interactive elements to convey how the brain constructs reality, themes that later informed his broader work on consciousness.[47] No major authored or edited books by Seth on AI or consciousness have appeared since 2021 as of late 2025.[47]

Awards and recognition

Scientific awards

Anil Seth has received several prestigious awards and honors recognizing his contributions to neuroscience, particularly in the study of consciousness and predictive processing. These accolades highlight the impact of his research on understanding the neural basis of conscious experience. In 2008, Seth was awarded an Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council (EPSRC) Leadership Fellowship, which supported his work in cognitive and computational neuroscience, enabling foundational investigations into perceptual inference and brain mechanisms of awareness.[1] From 2019 to 2024, Seth was named a Clarivate Highly Cited Researcher in neuroscience and neuropsychiatry, placing him in the top 0.1% of scientists globally based on the influence of his publications over the preceding decade.[2] In 2021, Seth was awarded a European Research Council (ERC) Advanced Grant of €2.43 million for the CONSCIOUS project (2021–2026), funding advanced empirical and theoretical research into the neural processes underlying conscious perception and selfhood.[23] Seth delivered the prestigious Segerfalk Lecture in 2023 at Lund University, an honor awarded for outstanding contributions to neuroscience research on brain function and cognition.[50]

Public communication awards

In 2023, Anil Seth was awarded the Royal Society Michael Faraday Prize and Lecture for his exceptional ability to communicate advances in cognitive neuroscience and consciousness to wide audiences, including through books, TED talks, and public lectures.[51] In 2014, Seth served as a consultant for the book Eye Benders: The Science of Seeing and Believing by Clive Gifford, which won the Royal Society Young People's Book Prize for its engaging explanation of visual perception and illusions to young readers.[2] In 2024, Seth was named one of Prospect Magazine's Top 25 Thinkers.[52] In 2025, Seth received the inaugural Rationalist Press Lecture Medal from Humanists UK for his contributions to public understanding of the human condition and consciousness from a naturalistic perspective, highlighted in his Voltaire Lecture on whether artificial intelligence could achieve consciousness.[53][54]

Public engagement

Media appearances

Anil Seth has appeared in several television documentaries and broadcasts exploring consciousness and perception. In the 2018 Netflix documentary The Most Unknown, directed by Ian Cheney, Seth featured as one of nine scientists discussing fundamental questions in science, including the nature of consciousness, with segments filmed around the University of Sussex.[55] He also appeared in the 2018 BBC Four documentary A neuroscientist explains why reality is not what it seems, where he elaborated on how the brain constructs perceptual experiences.[56] Additionally, Seth contributed to the 2022 BBC Ideas short film Why we all experience the world differently, addressing variations in human perception through neuroscience.[57] In print and online media, Seth has contributed essays and been interviewed on topics related to consciousness. He wrote a prominent 2016 essay for Aeon titled "The hard problem of consciousness is a distraction from the real one," arguing that scientific inquiry should prioritize the mechanisms of consciousness over philosophical puzzles.[58] As a regular contributor to The Guardian, Seth has authored pieces such as the 2022 "Big Idea" column on perceptual diversity.[59] Similarly, he has written for New Scientist, including a 2021 cover story "The hard problem of consciousness is already beginning to dissolve," which outlined a predictive processing approach to subjective experience.[60] Seth has been a frequent guest on podcasts and radio programs. On NPR's TED Radio Hour in 2016, he discussed how the brain constructs conscious reality through predictive mechanisms in the episode "How Does Your Brain Construct Your Conscious Reality?".[61] He has appeared multiple times on BBC Radio 4, including The Life Scientific in 2015, where he addressed the challenges of studying consciousness, and A Good Read in 2023, recommending books on neuroscience and psychology.[62][63] During the 2010s, Seth maintained the blog NeuroBanter, posting accessible explanations of neuroscience topics like ecstatic seizures and perceptual illusions.[64] Recent media coverage has highlighted Seth's talks on consciousness and AI. His April 2024 public lecture at the University of Helsinki, "Consciousness in humans and in other things," received attention in Finnish outlets like Helsingin Sanomat for its implications for AI sentience.[65] The October 2025 Voltaire Lecture, titled "What is consciousness, and could AI have it?", was covered by Humanists UK in an interview where Seth discussed biological bases of awareness and AI limitations.[66]

Science outreach and talks

Anil Seth has actively pursued science outreach through keynote lectures and public talks that make complex topics in consciousness accessible to non-specialist audiences. In October 2025, he delivered the Voltaire Lecture for Humanists UK at Conway Hall in London, titled "What is consciousness, and could AI have it?", where he outlined a predictive processing framework for understanding consciousness and its potential intersections with artificial intelligence.[67] This event, which drew a packed audience, was paired with the inaugural Rationalist Press Lecture Medal recognizing his contributions to rational inquiry into the mind.[54] Earlier, in April 2024, Seth presented a public talk at the University of Helsinki titled "Consciousness in humans and in other things," examining how consciousness emerges in biological systems and its implications for machines and non-human entities.[68] Seth's outreach extends to interactive initiatives that allow public participation in exploring perceptual and conscious experiences. He co-leads the Dreamachine project, a collaborative effort with artists, scientists, and philosophers that revives Brion Gysin's 1960s concept through modern installations using stroboscopic light to induce vivid hallucinations, enabling participants to experience the brain's constructive role in perception firsthand.[69] The project includes a citizen science component, the Perception Census, which collects data on subjective experiences to advance research while educating thousands on the illusory nature of reality.[36] Additionally, as director of the Sussex Centre for Consciousness Science, Seth oversees public-facing events such as the annual Sussex AI Day, which in 2024 and 2025 featured talks and workshops on consciousness, AI ethics, and neural prediction, attracting interdisciplinary audiences to discuss real-world applications of neuroscience.[70] In educational settings, Seth has targeted younger learners to inspire interest in cognitive science. For instance, in September 2025, he spoke at St Edward's School in Oxford on the science of consciousness and the prospects of AI sentience, emphasizing how brains "hallucinate" reality and why current AI lacks true awareness.[71] His broader educational efforts include his 2017 presidency of the British Science Association's Psychology Section, during which he organized lectures, panels, and festivals to promote public engagement with psychological research, including demonstrations of perceptual illusions and debates on the mind-brain relationship.[18] Through these activities, Seth has demystified consciousness for diverse audiences, blending rigorous science with experiential elements to illustrate how perceptions are actively constructed by the brain, fostering greater public appreciation of neuroscience's societal relevance.[24]
