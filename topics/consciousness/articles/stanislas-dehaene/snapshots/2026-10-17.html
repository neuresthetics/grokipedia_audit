Stanislas Dehaene

Fact-checked by Grok · 9 months ago
Stanislas Dehaene (born 12 May 1965) is a French cognitive neuroscientist renowned for his pioneering work on the brain's mechanisms supporting core human cognitive functions, including numerical intuition, reading acquisition, conscious awareness, and learning processes.[1] His research integrates neuroimaging, behavioral experiments, and computational modeling to elucidate how evolutionarily ancient brain circuits adapt to uniquely human abilities like arithmetic and literacy.[2] Dehaene's contributions have profoundly influenced fields such as cognitive psychology, neuroscience, and education, emphasizing the brain's plasticity and its implications for teaching and artificial intelligence.[3] Dehaene received his early training in mathematics at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, followed by a master's degree in applied mathematics from the University of Paris VI in 1985, and a PhD in cognitive science from the École des Hautes Études en Sciences Sociales in 1989.[1] After a postdoctoral fellowship at the University of Oregon from 1992 to 1994, he joined INSERM as a research scientist in 1989, advancing to research director of INSERM Unit 334 in Orsay (1999–2001) and then director of INSERM Unit 562 (2002–present).[1] In 2005, he was appointed to the Chair of Experimental Cognitive Psychology at the Collège de France, where he also serves as director of the NeuroSpin cognitive neuroimaging center at the Commissariat à l'énergie atomique et aux énergies alternatives (CEA).[2][4] Dehaene's seminal discoveries include the identification of dedicated brain circuits for number processing, such as the parietal lobe's role in representing quantity and parity, demonstrated through functional MRI and patient studies. He developed the global neuronal workspace theory of consciousness, positing that conscious access arises when sensory information is broadcast across a distributed frontoparietal network for global integration and reportability.[5] In reading research, he proposed the neuronal recycling hypothesis, explaining how visual cortex regions originally evolved for object recognition are repurposed for letter and word identification during literacy development.[2] His influential books, including The Number Sense (1997), Reading in the Brain (2009), and Consciousness and the Brain (2014), synthesize these findings for broad audiences, bridging neuroscience with philosophy and pedagogy.[6] Dehaene has received numerous prestigious awards for his interdisciplinary impact, including the 2014 Brain Prize from the Lundbeck Foundation for advancing understanding of cognitive brain circuits, the 2020 David E. Rumelhart Prize from the Cognitive Science Society, and the 2025 Lewis Thomas Prize for Writing about Science from The Rockefeller University.[7][8] He is a member of multiple academies, including the National Academy of Sciences, Academia Europaea, and the Pontifical Academy of Sciences, with an h-index exceeding 180 in psychology and neuroscience, reflecting over 100,000 citations to his work.[4][9]

Early life and education

Stanislas Dehaene was born on 12 May 1965 in Roubaix, France.[4] He received his early training in mathematics at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, graduating in 1984.[1] Dehaene earned a master's degree in applied mathematics from the University of Paris VI in 1985.[1] He then completed a PhD in cognitive science at the École des Hautes Études en Sciences Sociales in 1989, under the supervision of Jacques Mehler.[4]

Professional career

Academic appointments

Following his doctoral studies, Stanislas Dehaene returned to France and joined the French National Institute of Health and Medical Research (INSERM) as a research scientist in 1989 at the Laboratoire de Sciences Cognitives et Psycholinguistique in Paris, a position he held until 1999.[10] In 1992, he took a leave to pursue a postdoctoral fellowship at the Institute of Cognitive and Decision Sciences at the University of Oregon in the United States, where he worked under Michael Posner on neuroimaging techniques from 1992 to 1994.[10] Dehaene was promoted to Research Director at INSERM in 1997, a role he maintained until 2005 while continuing his affiliation with the institute. As Research Director, he led INSERM Unit 334 in Orsay from 1999 to 2001.[1] In September 2005, he was appointed Professor at the Collège de France, where he holds the inaugural Chair of Experimental Cognitive Psychology, succeeding Jean-Pierre Changeux in a newly created position dedicated to cognitive neuroscience.[2] In parallel with his INSERM directorship starting in 2002, Dehaene led the INSERM-CEA Cognitive Neuroimaging Unit (formerly INSERM Unit 562), initially based in Orsay until 2007 and then relocated to Saclay in 2008.[2][1] Since 2007, he has served as director of the NeuroSpin Center, a CEA facility for advanced brain imaging research at the Paris-Saclay site.[11][12] During the 1990s, Dehaene collaborated with Laurent Cohen on early brain imaging studies of language processing.[13]

Research leadership roles

Dehaene has directed the INSERM-CEA Cognitive Neuroimaging Unit (formerly INSERM Unit 562) since 2002, leading research on the neural mechanisms underlying human cognitive functions such as language, mathematics, and consciousness.[14] In 2007, he established the Cognitive Neuroimaging Unit affiliated with the Collège de France, integrating his professorial chair in Experimental Cognitive Psychology with advanced brain imaging initiatives.[2][15] Since 2007, Dehaene has served as director of NeuroSpin, a CEA research center specializing in cognitive brain imaging technologies and methodologies to explore high-level mental processes.[12][2] He has provided leadership in international collaborations, notably as an advisor to the Canadian Institute for Advanced Research (CIFAR)'s Brain, Mind & Consciousness program, fostering global dialogue on neural theories of awareness.[16] Since 2018, he has chaired the Scientific Council of National Education (CSEN) for the French Ministry of Education.[2] Through his laboratories, Dehaene has mentored over 60 PhD students and postdoctoral researchers, shaping the next generation of cognitive neuroscientists via hands-on training in experimental design and data analysis.[17] Under his direction, these institutions have pioneered advanced neuroimaging techniques, including high-resolution functional MRI, to map cognitive networks in the human brain.[14]

Research contributions

Numerical cognition

Dehaene's early work in numerical cognition, conducted in collaboration with Jacques Mehler during the 1990s, explored the cognitive representation of numbers through psycholinguistic and experimental paradigms. In one seminal study, they analyzed the frequency of number words across seven languages and cultures, revealing universal patterns in the usage of numerals and ordinals that suggested an innate bias toward low-number terms, with frequencies decreasing logarithmically as numbers increased. This cross-linguistic analysis indicated that such patterns might reflect evolutionary pressures on numerical language acquisition rather than arbitrary cultural conventions. Building on this, Dehaene and colleagues investigated spatial associations with numbers, identifying the spatial-numerical association of response codes (SNARC) effect, where participants respond faster to small numbers with left-hand keys and large numbers with right-hand keys, demonstrating a mental number line mapped onto left-right space. These findings, observed in tasks involving parity judgments and magnitude comparisons, provided initial evidence for an automatic, spatially organized representation of numerical magnitude independent of explicit training. Dehaene's neuroimaging research with Laurent Cohen further pinpointed the parietal lobe as a critical hub for arithmetic processing. Using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG), they demonstrated consistent activation in the bilateral intraparietal sulcus (IPS) during tasks involving number comparison, calculation, and estimation, suggesting this region underpins core numerical operations across formats. This work culminated in the triple-code model of number representation, which posits three distinct but interconnected codes: a verbal code for reciting number words (linked to left perisylvian language areas), an analog magnitude code for approximate quantities (anchored in the IPS), and an abstract Arabic code for symbolic manipulation (also involving parietal regions). The model, supported by lesion studies showing selective deficits in verbal versus magnitude-based tasks, explains how numbers are flexibly transformed between formats during computation. For instance, exact arithmetic like 17 + 25 = 42 relies more on verbal and abstract codes, while approximation engages the analog code for rapid, gist-like processing. In parallel, Dehaene developed computational models of numerical abilities with Jean-Pierre Changeux, simulating the emergence of number sense in neural networks. Their 1993 neuronal model used self-organizing maps to replicate infant-like discrimination of small numerosities (1-4 items) via subitizing and larger sets through summation coding, without explicit numerical input. The simulation showed how unstructured visual arrays could train an IPS-like module to encode approximate numerosity through divisive normalization, mirroring behavioral data from human infants who distinguish sets differing by at least two items at birth. This framework highlighted the innateness of basic number sense, evolving from primate-like object-tracking mechanisms into human symbolic arithmetic. A key distinction in Dehaene's findings concerns approximate versus exact arithmetic, both localized to the IPS but with nuanced differences. Approximate calculations, such as estimating 23 × 84 ≈ 1,800, activate the horizontal portion of the IPS bilaterally, supporting nonverbal, magnitude-based intuitions that align with logarithmic scaling observed in non-human animals and preverbal infants. In contrast, exact arithmetic recruits additional left-lateralized parietal and frontal areas for rule-based or memorized operations, as evidenced by fMRI studies where approximation tasks showed no performance cost in non-verbal contexts, unlike exact tasks that falter without language. These results underscore the IPS as a domain-general magnitude processor, handling not just numbers but also time and space, with approximate processing being more evolutionarily primitive. Dehaene's research extends to clinical and cultural applications, particularly developmental dyscalculia (DD), a disorder affecting 5-7% of children with core deficits in number sense. Neuroimaging in DD reveals reduced IPS connectivity and hypoactivation during magnitude tasks, akin to adult acalculia, supporting interventions like adaptive software (e.g., The Number Race) that train approximate arithmetic to bolster symbolic skills. Cross-culturally, Dehaene's studies with indigenous groups, such as the Munduruku in the Amazon, show that while exact arithmetic is culturally acquired and language-dependent, approximate number sense is universal, with IPS activations during estimation tasks comparable to Western participants despite lacking formal numerals beyond 5. This invariance across cultures reinforces the triple-code model's analog component as a biological foundation, modulated by linguistic input for precise counting.

Reading and language processing

Dehaene, in collaboration with Laurent Cohen, identified the visual word form area (VWFA), a specialized region in the left fusiform gyrus of the ventral visual stream, as a key neural substrate for early visual processing of written words. This area activates selectively for letter strings and words, independent of their retinal location, and exhibits invariance to variations in font, size, and case, allowing robust recognition across diverse typographic formats.[18][19] Functional neuroimaging studies by Dehaene demonstrated that the VWFA's specialization is acquired through literacy training, as illiterate adults show no selective activation in this region during exposure to written stimuli, unlike literate individuals who exhibit strong responses. This finding supports the view that reading repurposes existing visual circuits, consistent with the neuronal recycling hypothesis, whereby cultural inventions like writing adapt pre-existing brain networks for novel functions. In ex-illiterate adults who learn to read later in life, VWFA activation emerges, underscoring its dependence on experience rather than innate structure. Dehaene's research has delineated hierarchical processing within reading networks, where orthographic input in the VWFA feeds into phonological representations in superior temporal regions, such as the planum temporale, enabling the mapping of visual forms to speech sounds. Literacy enhances this integration, with top-down phonological predictions modulating orthographic processing and vice versa, forming a bidirectional circuit that strengthens with reading proficiency. These mechanisms ensure efficient decoding, where abstract letter identities are computed before phonetic assembly. Cross-linguistic fMRI investigations by Dehaene reveal a core reading network conserved across writing systems, including the left fusiform gyrus (VWFA), superior temporal gyrus, and frontal areas, despite differences between alphabetic scripts like French, which emphasize grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, and logographic ones like Chinese, which rely more on visual-semantic associations. In both, reading activates overlapping ventral and dorsal pathways, with script-specific modulations in activation strength but no fundamental reorganization of the underlying circuitry.[20] Dehaene's contributions to dyslexia research highlight disrupted connectivity in these reading circuits, particularly reduced functional coupling between the VWFA and phonological areas in the temporal lobe, leading to impaired orthographic-to-phonological translation. In dyslexic readers across languages, fMRI shows hypoactivation and anomalous synchronization in the left-hemisphere network, contributing to persistent decoding difficulties despite intact visual and auditory processing in isolation. These insights emphasize dyslexia as a connectivity disorder within the acquired reading system, informing targeted interventions.[20]

Consciousness and global neuronal workspace

Stanislas Dehaene, in collaboration with Jean-Pierre Changeux, developed the global neuronal workspace (GNW) theory in the late 1990s and early 2000s as a computational framework to explain the neural mechanisms underlying conscious access to information. The theory posits that consciousness arises from the broadcasting of selected sensory inputs across a distributed network of interconnected neurons, primarily involving prefrontal and parietal cortices, which act as a central "workspace" for integrating and amplifying relevant signals while suppressing irrelevant ones. This model, first formalized in a 1998 neuronal simulation, distinguishes between modular processors that handle unconscious computations and a global workspace that enables widespread access when activity reaches a critical ignition threshold.[21] Dehaene's empirical work has tested GNW predictions through experiments contrasting subliminal and conscious perception, often using visual masking paradigms where stimuli are presented below or above awareness thresholds. In these studies, subliminal stimuli elicit localized, feedforward activation in early sensory areas without propagating to higher-order regions, whereas conscious perception triggers an "ignition" event: a sudden, nonlinear surge in prefrontal-parietal activity that correlates with reportability and behavioral effects. Neuroimaging techniques, including fMRI and EEG, reveal this ignition as prolonged and widespread signaling, supporting GNW's claim that consciousness requires global integration rather than mere local processing. For instance, masking experiments demonstrate that only supraliminal stimuli produce late positivities in EEG (around 300 ms) and broad BOLD responses in fronto-parietal networks.[22]00088-1) To identify neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs), Dehaene employed contrastive analyses in fMRI and EEG, subtracting brain activity from unseen (subliminal) trials from seen (conscious) ones to isolate awareness-specific signatures. These analyses consistently highlight fronto-parietal ignition as the minimal NCC, with early sensory activations serving as precursors but insufficient for awareness alone. In clinical contexts, such as vegetative states, perturbations like TMS combined with EEG perturbational complexity index (PCI) have validated GNW by showing reduced global integration in unconscious patients.[23]30052-0) The GNW theory has faced critiques regarding methodological limitations in early contrastive paradigms, such as potential confounds from task demands or report biases that might inflate prefrontal involvement. Refinements, including no-report paradigms and adversarial testing against integrated information theory, have addressed these by emphasizing ignition's role in multiple content domains while acknowledging that consciousness may involve recurrent processing beyond simple broadcasting. In 2025, Dehaene and collaborators published findings from adversarial testing of GNW against integrated information theory, using multi-site fMRI and MEG to validate ignition signatures in conscious perception.[24]

Learning, education, and neuronal recycling

Dehaene's neuronal recycling hypothesis posits that cultural inventions, such as reading and arithmetic, repurpose existing brain circuits through exaptation, where evolutionarily older visual and cognitive areas are invaded and adapted for new functions without requiring major anatomical changes.[25] This process, detailed in his 2005 work, emphasizes the brain's bounded plasticity, allowing cultural evolution to harness pre-existing neural architectures for skills like number processing and literacy, as evidenced by cross-cultural consistencies in activated brain regions despite varying writing systems.[26] Subsequent refinements in 2007 highlighted how these recycled circuits inherit properties from their original functions, such as shape recognition in the ventral visual stream for letter identification.[27] Empirical support for neuronal recycling comes from neuroimaging studies tracking brain adaptations during learning. In longitudinal fMRI research, Dehaene's team examined the effects of brief educational interventions, such as viewing short videos on mathematical concepts, finding that while these videos engage intersubject synchrony and domain-general regions for short-term memory, they do not induce meaningful lasting changes in specialized mathematical networks like the intraparietal sulcus.[28] These findings illustrate the challenges of rapid neuronal reorganization, supporting the hypothesis that education recycles visual and attentional networks to build cultural competencies over time.[29] Dehaene has extended these insights into the science of learning, outlining a framework in his 2020 book How We Learn: Why Brains Learn Better Than Any Machine . . . for Now. He identifies four pillars of effective learning—attention, active engagement, error feedback, and consolidation—as the brain's core algorithm for acquiring knowledge, drawing on neural mechanisms like synaptic strengthening and sleep-based replay to optimize plasticity. This model underscores how educators can leverage neuronal recycling by focusing on these pillars to facilitate the repurposing of brain circuits for complex skills, such as mathematics, where error-driven feedback refines numerical intuitions.[30] In applying these principles to educational policy, Dehaene has advised on curriculum design in France. Appointed president of the Scientific Council for National Education in 2018, he has influenced reforms by advocating evidence-based approaches grounded in cognitive neuroscience, including recommendations for integrating active learning techniques to enhance brain adaptability in schools.[5] His role involves consulting on topics like teacher training and assessment, promoting policies that align with neuronal recycling to improve literacy and numeracy outcomes nationwide.[31] In the 2020s, Dehaene's work has explored the intersections of AI and human learning, highlighting the limits of brain adaptability in an era of machine-assisted education. He argues that while AI can augment teaching by providing personalized feedback, the brain's recycling capacity faces constraints from its fixed architecture, making it superior to current algorithms in efficient, few-shot learning but vulnerable to overload from excessive digital inputs.[32] Recent discussions emphasize designing AI tools that mimic the four pillars to respect these biological boundaries, ensuring technology supports rather than supplants natural neuronal processes.[33]

Awards and honors

Major scientific prizes

Stanislas Dehaene has received several prestigious international prizes recognizing his groundbreaking contributions to cognitive neuroscience, particularly in understanding human cognition, learning, and consciousness. In 2014, Dehaene shared The Brain Prize, awarded by the Lundbeck Foundation, with Giacomo Rizzolatti and Trevor Robbins for their pioneering research on the higher brain mechanisms that underpin complex human functions such as consciousness, language, and decision-making.[34] This €1 million award, one of the highest honors in neuroscience, highlighted Dehaene's work on how the brain acquires symbolic processes for mathematics and language, advancing the field of cognitive science.[35] In 2008, Dehaene received the Dr. A.H. Heineken Prize for Cognitive Science from the Royal Netherlands Academy of Sciences, recognizing his innovative research on the neural underpinnings of numerical cognition, reading, and conscious thought.[36] Dehaene received the Atkinson Prize in Psychological and Cognitive Sciences in 2024 from the U.S. National Academy of Sciences, shared with Vonnie C. McLoyd, for his seminal investigations into the neural and cognitive foundations of key human abilities including mathematical reasoning, reading acquisition, and conscious awareness.[37] The prize, which includes a medal and $25,000, underscores Dehaene's integration of neuroimaging, behavioral experiments, and theoretical models to elucidate brain mechanisms in these domains.[38] In 2020, Dehaene was awarded the David E. Rumelhart Prize by the Cognitive Science Society for his distinguished contributions to the theoretical foundations of human cognition, particularly in numerical intuition, literacy, and consciousness.[39] In 2025, Dehaene was awarded the Lewis Thomas Prize for Writing about Science by The Rockefeller University, honoring his exceptional ability to communicate complex neuroscience concepts to broad audiences through books and lectures that bridge science and the humanities.[40] This annual prize, named after the physician and essayist Lewis Thomas, recognizes Dehaene's popular works like Consciousness and the Brain for making the intricacies of brain function accessible and inspiring.[8] Also in 2025, Dehaene earned the Research.com Best Scientists Award, acknowledging his profound influence in psychology and neuroscience based on metrics such as his h-index of 184 and over 150,000 citations, positioning him as a leading figure in France and globally.[9] This accolade from the Research.com ranking platform emphasizes the broad impact of his research on neuronal recycling, numerical cognition, and global workspace theory.[41]

Fellowships and distinctions

Dehaene's contributions to cognitive neuroscience have earned him election to several prestigious academies, reflecting recognition by his international peers for advancing understanding of human cognition through brain imaging and behavioral studies. He became a Foreign Associate of the National Academy of Sciences of the United States in 2010, joining an elite group dedicated to advancing scientific knowledge and advising on policy matters.[42] In 2023, Dehaene was elected a Foreign Member of the Royal Society, the United Kingdom's oldest and most prestigious scientific academy, in acknowledgment of his pioneering work on the neural bases of consciousness and learning.[11] Within France, Dehaene received the title of Chevalier de la Légion d'Honneur in 2011, the nation's highest civil honor, bestowed for exceptional service to the republic in fields including science and education.[10] He was promoted to Officer of the Légion d'Honneur in 2020, recognizing sustained leadership in national scientific endeavors.[43] He also holds the rank of Chevalier in the Ordre national du Mérite, awarded in 2008.[10] Additionally, in 2008, Dehaene was elected a member of the Pontifical Academy of Sciences, a body that promotes scientific progress in dialogue with ethical and philosophical considerations.[4] These fellowships underscore his role in bridging experimental psychology with broader interdisciplinary impacts on education and consciousness research.

Publications

Books as author

Stanislas Dehaene's first major monograph, The Number Sense: How the Mind Creates Mathematics, published in 1997 by Oxford University Press, explores the cognitive and neural foundations of numerical cognition, arguing that humans possess an innate "number sense" for approximate quantities that evolves through learning to support exact arithmetic and advanced mathematics.[44] The book draws on psychological experiments, neuroimaging studies, and cross-species comparisons to demonstrate how this primitive intuition, shared with animals, underpins human mathematical abilities while highlighting limitations that education must overcome.[45] Widely influential in cognitive science, the work has been cited over 7,400 times as of 2025 and translated into at least eight languages, shaping research on early numerical development and educational interventions.[6][46] Dehaene's 2009 book Reading in the Brain: The New Science of How We Read, published by Viking, details the neuronal recycling hypothesis, explaining how evolutionarily ancient visual circuits are repurposed for reading and literacy acquisition. It integrates brain imaging, patient studies, and cross-linguistic data to reveal dedicated networks like the visual word form area, influencing education and dyslexia research, with over 3,500 citations as of 2025.[47][6] In Consciousness and the Brain: Deciphering How the Brain Codes Our Thoughts, released in 2014 by Viking Press, Dehaene synthesizes decades of experimental evidence to popularize the global neuronal workspace (GNW) theory, positing that consciousness arises when sensory information is broadcast across a distributed brain network for global integration and reportability.[48] Through discussions of masking experiments, brain imaging, and clinical cases like blindsight, the book illustrates how unconscious processing handles routine tasks while conscious access enables flexible deliberation, challenging dualist views with empirical neuroscience.[49] Cited more than 1,950 times as of 2025, it has advanced interdisciplinary debates on consciousness and been translated into multiple languages as part of Dehaene's broader oeuvre reaching fifteen tongues.[6][50] Dehaene's book How We Learn: Why Brains Learn Better Than Any Machine . . . for Now (English edition 2020 by Viking, originally published in French in 2018 as Apprendre: Le cerveau éduqué by Odile Jacob), outlines brain-based principles of learning derived from his neuronal recycling hypothesis, emphasizing four pillars—attention, active engagement, error feedback, and consolidation—to optimize education. It contrasts human learning's adaptability with artificial intelligence, advocating reforms like spaced repetition and sleep's role in memory, supported by fMRI data and developmental studies.[51] The volume, cited in educational neuroscience contexts, promotes evidence-based pedagogy and has contributed to global discussions on reforming teaching practices.[52][53]

Edited works and key articles

Dehaene co-edited Numerical Cognition, a collection published in 1993 by Blackwell that compiled foundational computational and experimental models of numerical processing from leading researchers in the field.[54] This volume emphasized early theoretical frameworks for understanding how the brain represents and manipulates numbers, influencing subsequent work in cognitive neuroscience.[54][55] In 2001, Dehaene edited The Cognitive Neuroscience of Consciousness, a seminal anthology published by MIT Press that integrated philosophical, empirical, and theoretical perspectives on the neural basis of conscious experience, including contributions from key figures in the field.[56] The book laid groundwork for the global neuronal workspace theory by synthesizing evidence from neuroimaging and behavioral studies on conscious access.[56] Among Dehaene's influential articles, his 2001 paper "Towards a cognitive neuroscience of consciousness: basic evidence and a workspace framework," co-authored with Lionel Naccache and published in Cognition, proposed a framework distinguishing unconscious from conscious processing and introduced the global workspace model as a mechanism for accessing awareness.[57] This work has been widely cited for bridging psychological and neural accounts of access consciousness.[57] Another key contribution is the 2000 article "The visual word form area: spatial and temporal characterization of an fMRI response," co-authored with Laurent Cohen and others in Neuroreport, which identified a specialized region in the left occipitotemporal cortex responsive to written words, independent of lexical meaning.[58] This paper established the visual word form area (VWFA) as a critical node in reading networks through fMRI evidence from healthy subjects.[58] In 2017, Dehaene co-authored "What is consciousness, and could machines have it?" with Hakwan Lau and Sid Kouider, published in Science, reviewing neural signatures of consciousness and evaluating whether computational systems could replicate them.[59] The article argues for four indicator properties of consciousness—richness, globality, integration, and reportability—and assesses AI architectures against them, highlighting gaps in current machine learning models.[59] Dehaene served as associate editor of the journal Cognition from 1999 to 2005, overseeing submissions on cognitive psychology and neuroscience topics.[2] He has also held editorial roles on boards of journals such as NeuroImage since 2001.[2]

Personal life

Dehaene is married to the neuroscientist Ghislaine Dehaene-Lambertz.[60] Together, they have three sons.[61] His father was a pediatrician.[60]
