### Numerical cognition

**C0032** Dehaene's early work in numerical cognition, conducted in collaboration with Jacques Mehler during the 1990s, explored the cognitive representation of numbers through psycholinguistic and experimental paradigms. **C0033** In one seminal study, they analyzed the frequency of number words across seven languages and cultures, revealing universal patterns in the usage of numerals and ordinals that suggested an innate bias toward low-number terms, with frequencies decreasing logarithmically as numbers increased. **C0034** This cross-linguistic analysis indicated that such patterns might reflect evolutionary pressures on numerical language acquisition rather than arbitrary cultural conventions. **C0035** Building on this, Dehaene and colleagues investigated spatial associations with numbers, identifying the spatial-numerical association of response codes (SNARC) effect, where participants respond faster to small numbers with left-hand keys and large numbers with right-hand keys, demonstrating a mental number line mapped onto left-right space. **C0036** These findings, observed in tasks involving parity judgments and magnitude comparisons, provided initial evidence for an automatic, spatially organized representation of numerical magnitude independent of explicit training.

**C0037** Dehaene's neuroimaging research with Laurent Cohen further pinpointed the parietal lobe as a critical hub for arithmetic processing. **C0038** Using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and electroencephalography (EEG), they demonstrated consistent activation in the bilateral intraparietal sulcus (IPS) during tasks involving number comparison, calculation, and estimation, suggesting this region underpins core numerical operations across formats. **C0039** This work culminated in the triple-code model of number representation, which posits three distinct but interconnected codes: a verbal code for reciting number words (linked to left perisylvian language areas), an analog magnitude code for approximate quantities (anchored in the IPS), and an abstract Arabic code for symbolic manipulation (also involving parietal regions). **C0040** The model, supported by lesion studies showing selective deficits in verbal versus magnitude-based tasks, explains how numbers are flexibly transformed between formats during computation. **C0041** For instance, exact arithmetic like 17 + 25 = 42 relies more on verbal and abstract codes, while approximation engages the analog code for rapid, gist-like processing.

**C0042** In parallel, Dehaene developed computational models of numerical abilities with Jean-Pierre Changeux, simulating the emergence of number sense in neural networks. **C0043** Their 1993 neuronal model used self-organizing maps to replicate infant-like discrimination of small numerosities (1-4 items) via subitizing and larger sets through summation coding, without explicit numerical input. **C0044** The simulation showed how unstructured visual arrays could train an IPS-like module to encode approximate numerosity through divisive normalization, mirroring behavioral data from human infants who distinguish sets differing by at least two items at birth. **C0045** This framework highlighted the innateness of basic number sense, evolving from primate-like object-tracking mechanisms into human symbolic arithmetic.

**C0046** A key distinction in Dehaene's findings concerns approximate versus exact arithmetic, both localized to the IPS but with nuanced differences. **C0047** Approximate calculations, such as estimating 23 × 84 ≈ 1,800, activate the horizontal portion of the IPS bilaterally, supporting nonverbal, magnitude-based intuitions that align with logarithmic scaling observed in non-human animals and preverbal infants. **C0048** In contrast, exact arithmetic recruits additional left-lateralized parietal and frontal areas for rule-based or memorized operations, as evidenced by fMRI studies where approximation tasks showed no performance cost in non-verbal contexts, unlike exact tasks that falter without language. **C0049** These results underscore the IPS as a domain-general magnitude processor, handling not just numbers but also time and space, with approximate processing being more evolutionarily primitive.

**C0050** Dehaene's research extends to clinical and cultural applications, particularly developmental dyscalculia (DD), a disorder affecting 5-7% of children with core deficits in number sense. **C0051** Neuroimaging in DD reveals reduced IPS connectivity and hypoactivation during magnitude tasks, akin to adult acalculia, supporting interventions like adaptive software (e.g., The Number Race) that train approximate arithmetic to bolster symbolic skills. **C0052** Cross-culturally, Dehaene's studies with indigenous groups, such as the Munduruku in the Amazon, show that while exact arithmetic is culturally acquired and language-dependent, approximate number sense is universal, with IPS activations during estimation tasks comparable to Western participants despite lacking formal numerals beyond 5. **C0053** This invariance across cultures reinforces the triple-code model's analog component as a biological foundation, modulated by linguistic input for precise counting.

### Reading and language processing

**C0054** Dehaene, in collaboration with Laurent Cohen, identified the visual word form area (VWFA), a specialized region in the left fusiform gyrus of the ventral visual stream, as a key neural substrate for early visual processing of written words. **C0055** This area activates selectively for letter strings and words, independent of their retinal location, and exhibits invariance to variations in font, size, and case, allowing robust recognition across diverse typographic formats. [18] [19]

**C0056** Functional neuroimaging studies by Dehaene demonstrated that the VWFA's specialization is acquired through literacy training, as illiterate adults show no selective activation in this region during exposure to written stimuli, unlike literate individuals who exhibit strong responses. **C0057** This finding supports the view that reading repurposes existing visual circuits, consistent with the neuronal recycling hypothesis, whereby cultural inventions like writing adapt pre-existing brain networks for novel functions. **C0058** In ex-illiterate adults who learn to read later in life, VWFA activation emerges, underscoring its dependence on experience rather than innate structure.

**C0059** Dehaene's research has delineated hierarchical processing within reading networks, where orthographic input in the VWFA feeds into phonological representations in superior temporal regions, such as the planum temporale, enabling the mapping of visual forms to speech sounds. **C0060** Literacy enhances this integration, with top-down phonological predictions modulating orthographic processing and vice versa, forming a bidirectional circuit that strengthens with reading proficiency. **C0061** These mechanisms ensure efficient decoding, where abstract letter identities are computed before phonetic assembly.

**C0062** Cross-linguistic fMRI investigations by Dehaene reveal a core reading network conserved across writing systems, including the left fusiform gyrus (VWFA), superior temporal gyrus, and frontal areas, despite differences between alphabetic scripts like French, which emphasize grapheme-to-phoneme conversion, and logographic ones like Chinese, which rely more on visual-semantic associations. **C0063** In both, reading activates overlapping ventral and dorsal pathways, with script-specific modulations in activation strength but no fundamental reorganization of the underlying circuitry. [20]

**C0064** Dehaene's contributions to dyslexia research highlight disrupted connectivity in these reading circuits, particularly reduced functional coupling between the VWFA and phonological areas in the temporal lobe, leading to impaired orthographic-to-phonological translation. **C0065** In dyslexic readers across languages, fMRI shows hypoactivation and anomalous synchronization in the left-hemisphere network, contributing to persistent decoding difficulties despite intact visual and auditory processing in isolation. **C0066** These insights emphasize dyslexia as a connectivity disorder within the acquired reading system, informing targeted interventions. [20]

### Consciousness and global neuronal workspace

**C0067** Stanislas Dehaene, in collaboration with Jean-Pierre Changeux, developed the global neuronal workspace (GNW) theory in the late 1990s and early 2000s as a computational framework to explain the neural mechanisms underlying conscious access to information. **C0068** The theory posits that consciousness arises from the broadcasting of selected sensory inputs across a distributed network of interconnected neurons, primarily involving prefrontal and parietal cortices, which act as a central "workspace" for integrating and amplifying relevant signals while suppressing irrelevant ones. **C0069** This model, first formalized in a 1998 neuronal simulation, distinguishes between modular processors that handle unconscious computations and a global workspace that enables widespread access when activity reaches a critical ignition threshold. [21]

**C0070** Dehaene's empirical work has tested GNW predictions through experiments contrasting subliminal and conscious perception, often using visual masking paradigms where stimuli are presented below or above awareness thresholds. **C0071** In these studies, subliminal stimuli elicit localized, feedforward activation in early sensory areas without propagating to higher-order regions, whereas conscious perception triggers an "ignition" event: a sudden, nonlinear surge in prefrontal-parietal activity that correlates with reportability and behavioral effects. **C0072** Neuroimaging techniques, including fMRI and EEG, reveal this ignition as prolonged and widespread signaling, supporting GNW's claim that consciousness requires global integration rather than mere local processing. **C0073** For instance, masking experiments demonstrate that only supraliminal stimuli produce late positivities in EEG (around 300 ms) and broad BOLD responses in fronto-parietal networks.00088-1) [22]

### Learning, education, and neuronal recycling

**C0080** Dehaene's neuronal recycling hypothesis posits that cultural inventions, such as reading and arithmetic, repurpose existing brain circuits through exaptation, where evolutionarily older visual and cognitive areas are invaded and adapted for new functions without requiring major anatomical changes. [25] **C0081** This process, detailed in his 2005 work, emphasizes the brain's bounded plasticity, allowing cultural evolution to harness pre-existing neural architectures for skills like number processing and literacy, as evidenced by cross-cultural consistencies in activated brain regions despite varying writing systems. [26] **C0082** Subsequent refinements in 2007 highlighted how these recycled circuits inherit properties from their original functions, such as shape recognition in the ventral visual stream for letter identification. [27]

**C0083** Empirical support for neuronal recycling comes from neuroimaging studies tracking brain adaptations during learning. **C0084** In longitudinal fMRI research, Dehaene's team examined the effects of brief educational interventions, such as viewing short videos on mathematical concepts, finding that while these videos engage intersubject synchrony and domain-general regions for short-term memory, they do not induce meaningful lasting changes in specialized mathematical networks like the intraparietal sulcus. [28] **C0085** These findings illustrate the challenges of rapid neuronal reorganization, supporting the hypothesis that education recycles visual and attentional networks to build cultural competencies over time. [29]

**C0086** Dehaene has extended these insights into the science of learning, outlining a framework in his 2020 book How We Learn: Why Brains Learn Better Than Any Machine . . . for Now. **C0087** He identifies four pillars of effective learning—attention, active engagement, error feedback, and consolidation—as the brain's core algorithm for acquiring knowledge, drawing on neural mechanisms like synaptic strengthening and sleep-based replay to optimize plasticity. **C0088** This model underscores how educators can leverage neuronal recycling by focusing on these pillars to facilitate the repurposing of brain circuits for complex skills, such as mathematics, where error-driven feedback refines numerical intuitions. [30]

**C0089** In applying these principles to educational policy, Dehaene has advised on curriculum design in France. **C0090** Appointed president of the Scientific Council for National Education in 2018, he has influenced reforms by advocating evidence-based approaches grounded in cognitive neuroscience, including recommendations for integrating active learning techniques to enhance brain adaptability in schools. [5] **C0091** His role involves consulting on topics like teacher training and assessment, promoting policies that align with neuronal recycling to improve literacy and numeracy outcomes nationwide. [31]

**C0092** In the 2020s, Dehaene's work has explored the intersections of AI and human learning, highlighting the limits of brain adaptability in an era of machine-assisted education. **C0093** He argues that while AI can augment teaching by providing personalized feedback, the brain's recycling capacity faces constraints from its fixed architecture, making it superior to current algorithms in efficient, few-shot learning but vulnerable to overload from excessive digital inputs. [32] **C0094** Recent discussions emphasize designing AI tools that mimic the four pillars to respect these biological boundaries, ensuring technology supports rather than supplants natural neuronal processes.
