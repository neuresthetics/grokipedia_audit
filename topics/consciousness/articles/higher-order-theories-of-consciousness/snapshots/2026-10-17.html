Higher-order theories of consciousness

Fact-checked by Grok · 9 months ago
Higher-order theories of consciousness are a family of philosophical and neuroscientific accounts proposing that a mental state is conscious if and only if it is accompanied by a higher-order representation—such as a thought, perception, or other meta-representation—of that state itself, thereby enabling subjective awareness.[1] This framework, often grounded in the transitivity principle, asserts that whenever a mental state is conscious, an individual is aware of being in that state through some suitable higher-order process, distinguishing it from unconscious mental activity that lacks such representation.[2] Ideas akin to higher-order theories appear in early modern philosophers like Locke, who emphasized reflection on mental states as central to consciousness, with related discussions of self-awareness by Descartes, but the modern formulation emerged in the late 20th century amid growing recognition of unconscious perception in psychology and neuroscience.[1] David Rosenthal formalized the higher-order thought (HOT) variant in the 1980s and 1990s, arguing that consciousness arises from noninferential, conceptual thoughts about one's first-order mental states, which confer reportability and subjective character without requiring explicit introspection.[3] Parallel developments include David Armstrong's and William Lycan's higher-order perception (HOP) models from the 1960s onward, which analogize inner awareness to sensory monitoring mechanisms that "perceive" mental states as if they were external objects.[1] Key variants of higher-order theories include dispositional HOTs, proposed by Peter Carruthers, which treat higher-order states as potential rather than actual representations to accommodate cases like sleep or infancy where explicit thoughts may be absent.[2] Other extensions, such as Rocco Gennaro's and Uriah Kriegel's wide intrinsic HOTs, integrate self-representational elements where mental states inherently contain higher-order content about themselves, addressing qualia and first-person perspectives.[1] In neuroscience, applications like Joseph LeDoux's higher-order theory of emotional consciousness (HOTEC) extend the model to affective states, positing that conscious emotions require prefrontal and parietal cortical re-representations of subcortical signals for integration into a unified subjective experience. Higher-order theories offer explanatory advantages in accounting for phenomena like blindsight, where patients process visual information unconsciously without higher-order access, and change blindness, where scene alterations go unnoticed due to absent meta-representations.[1] Empirical support draws from neuroimaging studies showing prefrontal activation correlates with conscious reportability over mere sensory processing (Lau & Rosenthal, 2011), and behavioral experiments demonstrating that confidence judgments—hallmarks of higher-order monitoring—predict conscious perception.[4] These theories contrast with first-order representationalism, which attributes consciousness directly to sensory content, by emphasizing cognitive re-representation as essential for phenomenal experience.[4] Criticisms of higher-order theories include the misrepresentation objection, raised by Ned Block and others, which argues that erroneous higher-order states could generate illusory consciousness without genuine phenomenology, as in radical cases of confabulation.[1] An infinite regress concern posits that higher-order states themselves require further meta-representations to be conscious, though proponents counter that such states need not be conscious to confer consciousness on targets.[4] Recent debates, including those in recent adversarial collaborations such as the 2023 protocols and the 2024 ETHOS project, highlight ongoing challenges in distinguishing higher-order models from global workspace or integrated information theories, with calls for more precise neural predictions. Ongoing efforts include the ETHOS project (2024–present), an adversarial collaboration empirically testing higher-order theories against first-order theories.[1][5] Despite these issues, higher-order approaches remain influential for bridging philosophical introspection with empirical findings on metacognition and self-awareness.[1]

Overview

Core principles

Higher-order theories of consciousness (HOTs) posit that a mental state becomes conscious only if it is represented by a distinct higher-order mental state that makes one aware of being in that state.[1] This awareness serves as the necessary condition for consciousness, distinguishing conscious experiences from unconscious mental processes.[1] First-order mental states refer to primary perceptual, cognitive, or affective states that occur without self-reference, such as the unconscious registration of visual changes in a scene during change blindness experiments, where the visual system processes information but lacks subjective awareness.[1] Higher-order states, by contrast, are meta-representational processes—often involving attention or monitoring—that target and re-represent these first-order states, thereby elevating them to consciousness; for example, directing attention to a visual scene can transform an unconscious perceptual input into a conscious experience of noticing a change.[1] At the core of HOTs is the mechanism of meta-mental representation, where consciousness does not inhere intrinsically in a mental state but emerges relationally through a higher-order state's targeting of it, guided by the transitivity principle that a state M1 is conscious if and only if there is awareness of being in M1 via a higher-order state M2.[1] This representational approach underscores that phenomenal experience arises from the structure of mental self-reference rather than from the content or neural basis of the first-order state alone.[1] HOTs include a brief taxonomy of variants, such as higher-order perceptual theories, which construe awareness as a form of inner perception of mental states; higher-order thought theories, which involve conceptual thoughts about those states; and self-representational theories, where the higher-order element is embedded within the first-order state itself.[1] Unlike global workspace theory, which emphasizes the broadcasting of first-order information across neural networks for consciousness, HOTs prioritize the higher-order representational relation as the key to subjective awareness.[6]

Historical development

The roots of higher-order theories of consciousness (HOTs) can be traced to early philosophical discussions of introspection as a form of internal monitoring. In his 1690 An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, John Locke described reflection as an internal operation of the mind that perceives its own ideas, positing it as a secondary source of knowledge alongside sensation, which prefigures the idea of higher-order awareness of first-order mental states.[7] This notion of self-monitoring gained materialist traction in the 20th century, particularly through David M. Armstrong's 1968 A Materialist Theory of the Mind, where he proposed that introspection involves a "scanning" mechanism by which the mind perceives its own states, akin to an inner sense that detects mental occurrences without invoking immaterial faculties.[8] The modern formulation of HOTs emerged in the late 20th century with David M. Rosenthal's work, which systematically distinguished between creature consciousness (wakefulness) and state consciousness (awareness of specific mental states), arguing that the latter requires higher-order thoughts about first-order states. Rosenthal's seminal 1986 paper, "Two Concepts of Consciousness," laid the groundwork by introducing the higher-order thought (HOT) hypothesis, positing that a mental state is conscious only if accompanied by a suitable higher-order thought representing it as currently occurring.[9] He further developed this in his 1993 article "Higher-Order Thoughts and the Appendage Theory of Consciousness," refining the theory to address objections regarding the nature of these meta-representations and their role in phenomenal experience. Key milestones in the 1980s and 1990s expanded HOTs into variants emphasizing perceptual or functional aspects. William G. Lycan's 1987 book Consciousness advanced an "inner sense" theory, portraying consciousness as a higher-order perceptual monitoring system that scans first-order states, drawing on Armstrong's scanning idea but integrating it with functionalist frameworks.[10] Lycan elaborated this in his 1996 Consciousness and Experience, defending it against dualist critiques by emphasizing its compatibility with physicalism.[11] In the 2000s, Peter Carruthers introduced a dispositional variant in his 2000 Phenomenal Consciousness: A Naturalistic Theory, arguing that consciousness arises from the disposition to form higher-order thoughts rather than their actual occurrence, allowing for broader applicability to non-linguistic creatures.[12] Post-2000 developments integrated HOTs with cognitive science, sparking debates on consciousness in animals during the 2010s, where proponents like Carruthers argued that dispositional HOTs could extend phenomenal consciousness to many non-human species without requiring conceptual thought. Uriah Kriegel's 2009 Subjective Consciousness: A Self-Representational Theory marked a shift toward self-representational HOTs, proposing that conscious states inherently represent themselves, unifying first- and higher-order elements within a single state to address worries about representational overflow.[13] Recent refinements from 2023 to 2025, including discussions on PhilSci-Archive, have explored HO representations in AI models, examining whether large language models exhibit introspective-like meta-representations that mimic HOT mechanisms, informing ethical considerations for artificial systems.[14]

Types

Higher-order perception theory

Higher-order perception (HOP) theory maintains that a mental state becomes conscious when it is the object of a higher-order perceptual representation, functioning as an "inner sense" that monitors first-order states in a manner analogous to external sensory perception. This approach treats consciousness not as a fundamental property but as arising from perceptual relations within the mind, where the higher-order perception directly targets and represents the lower-order state, such as a sensation of color or pain. William Lycan has been a primary architect of this view, elaborating it in his 1987 book Consciousness and further refining it in Consciousness and Experience (1996), where he argues that such higher-order perceptions explain why certain mental states feel subjectively present to the individual.[15][16] The mechanism of HOP theory involves a dedicated higher-order perceptual module or "inner sense" that scans and represents first-order mental states, much like the visual system perceives external objects. For example, a first-order state of pain is rendered conscious only if it is perceived by this inner sense, producing a perceptual experience of the pain itself rather than a mere abstract judgment about it. This perceptual framing distinguishes HOP from other higher-order approaches by emphasizing sensory-like qualities in the monitoring process, avoiding reliance on linguistic or conceptual content. Lycan (1996) describes this inner sense as a functional component of the cognitive architecture, capable of representing first-order states with their intrinsic properties intact.[16] One key advantage of HOP theory is its ability to account for the phenomenal feel or qualia of conscious experiences through the familiar analogy to sensory perception, suggesting that the "what it's like" aspect stems from the perceptual nature of the higher-order representation rather than from the first-order state alone. This avoids the abstraction inherent in thought-based models, providing a more intuitive explanation for why conscious states possess vivid, immediate presence. Additionally, by modeling awareness as perception, the theory aligns with everyday intuitions about seeing or feeling one's own mental states, as Lycan (1987) contends.[15] HOP theory finds illustrative support in phenomena like blindsight, where patients with damage to primary visual cortex can respond to visual stimuli without conscious awareness, interpreted as a failure of the higher-order inner sense to perceive the intact first-order visual processing. In such cases, the absence of higher-order perception results in unconscious vision, highlighting the necessity of the inner sense for phenomenal consciousness. Lycan (1996) uses this example to underscore how disruptions in higher-order monitoring can dissociate processing from awareness.[16][17] An early variant of HOP theory appears in David Armstrong's (1968) "scan" model, which posits consciousness as a self-scanning process within the central nervous system, where one neural subsystem perceptually scans another to produce awareness of mental states. Armstrong describes this as a materialist mechanism akin to introspection via internal perception, laying groundwork for later HOP developments by framing consciousness as perceptual monitoring rather than epiphenomenal.[18] In contrast to higher-order thought theories, which rely on occurrent thoughts for awareness, HOP theory prioritizes perceptual representations to capture the sensory essence of consciousness.[17]

Actualist higher-order thought theory

Actualist higher-order thought theory posits that a mental state is conscious only if it is accompanied by an actual, occurrent higher-order thought (HOT) about that state.[9] This view, developed by David Rosenthal, distinguishes between conscious and unconscious mental states by requiring the presence of a contemporaneous HOT that represents the subject as being in the first-order state, without relying on inference or additional sensory input.[9] In this framework, the HOT functions as a metarepresentational thought that targets the first-order mental state, thereby conferring consciousness upon it.[19] For instance, a perceptual state such as seeing something red becomes conscious through an accompanying HOT like "I am seeing something red," which makes the first-order state available for rational control and reportability.[19] These HOTs are themselves typically nonconscious, allowing consciousness to arise without deliberate attention or effort.[19] A central claim of the theory is that unconscious mental states lack such accompanying HOTs, and any potential for consciousness remains merely dispositional until an actual HOT occurs.[9] Thus, states like beliefs or desires can influence behavior nonconsciously if no HOT represents them.[9] Illustrative examples include subliminal priming, where brief visual stimuli affect subsequent judgments without generating an HOT, leaving the perceptual state unconscious.[9] In contrast, introspection involves deliberately focusing on a mental state, which prompts the formation of an HOT—such as thinking "I am now thinking that it is raining"—rendering the state conscious and enabling its verbal report.[19] The theory also implies that targetless HOTs, which represent nonexistent first-order states, can nonetheless produce conscious experiences, as the consciousness derives from the HOT's representational content rather than the target's actual occurrence.[20] Such cases, though rare and often pathological, underscore the representational nature of consciousness in this account.[20]

Dispositional higher-order thought theory

The dispositional higher-order thought (DHOT) theory posits that a first-order mental state becomes conscious when the subject possesses a disposition to form a higher-order thought (HOT) about that state under suitable conditions, rather than requiring the actual occurrence of such a thought. This view, developed by Peter Carruthers, contrasts with actualist HOT theories by emphasizing potentiality over actuality in the higher-order representation of experience.[21] In DHOT, consciousness arises through implicit monitoring systems that enable the first-order state to be globally available for higher-order representation, often without necessitating an explicit or conscious HOT; for instance, attentional mechanisms can suffice to establish this dispositional readiness. The theory relies on consumer semantics, where the content of a mental state is determined by the cognitive systems poised to consume it, such as a theory-of-mind module that could generate a HOT like "I am seeing green" if prompted. Actual HOTs are thus not required for consciousness, as the disposition itself confers the phenomenal character via dual-content representation—combining first-order sensory information with higher-order dispositional awareness.[21] One key advantage of DHOT is its ability to account for conscious experience in creatures with limited metacognitive abilities, such as human infants and non-human animals, by focusing on the underlying capacity for HOTs rather than their overt expression or linguistic articulation. This dispositional approach avoids the cognitive overhead of constant higher-order monitoring, making it more parsimonious for explaining everyday phenomenal awareness without invoking resource-intensive inner-sense mechanisms. Examples of DHOT in action include cases of confident perceptual judgments, such as recognizing an object without an accompanying explicit thought of "I think that I am perceiving this," where the disposition to form such a HOT renders the experience conscious; this accommodates simple forms of awareness that actualist theories might exclude as non-conscious. In contrast to actualist HOT views, which demand an occurrent higher-order representation and thus limit consciousness to states with active self-monitoring, DHOT broadens the scope to include dispositional potentials.[21] DHOT relates to expressivism in that HOTs function as interpretive expressions of underlying dispositions toward self-representation, rather than as direct causal agents of consciousness; conscious states are thus manifested through the potential for such expressions in behavior or judgment, aligning with naturalistic accounts of mental content.

Self-representational higher-order theories

Self-representational higher-order theories propose that a mental state becomes conscious through an intrinsic higher-order representation of itself embedded within the state, rather than through a distinct meta-representational mechanism. In this view, consciousness arises from the state's partial self-directed intentionality, where the same mental event serves both as a first-order representation of the world and as a higher-order representation of that first-order content. This framework was notably articulated by Robert Van Gulick, who integrated self-representation into global state models of consciousness, and further developed by Uriah Kriegel, who argued that subjective character—the for-me-ness of experience—is constituted by such self-representational structure. The mechanism underlying these theories involves a hierarchical organization within the conscious state itself. For example, a perceptual state might consist of a core representation of an external object combined with a subordinate representation that targets the core, thereby making the entire state self-aware without invoking an external "inner sense" or monitoring system. This intrinsic embedding distinguishes self-representational approaches from other higher-order variants, as it eliminates the need for separate higher-order states that could lead to explanatory gaps or dual systems. Van Gulick's higher-order global states (HOGS) model exemplifies this by positing that consciousness emerges from the integration of first-order content with self-referential aspects at a global level, ensuring the state's reflexivity is part of its constitutive architecture.[22] A key advantage of self-representational theories is their resolution of the infinite regress problem inherent in traditional higher-order accounts, where each higher-order thought seemingly requires its own higher-order representation. By locating the higher-order component immanently within the target state, these theories halt the regress, as the self-representation is non-derivative and does not demand further meta-level awareness. Furthermore, this structure explains the unity of consciousness: the state's self-representation binds its diverse elements (such as sensory qualities and temporal features) into a single, coherent phenomenal whole accessible to the subject in a first-personal manner. Kriegel emphasizes that this unity is not imposed externally but arises from the state's holistic self-presentation, aligning with phenomenological descriptions of experiences as inherently subjective and integrated.[22] These theories apply to a range of conscious states beyond perception. For pains, the experience includes an embedded representation of its own aversive quality, rendering it immediately self-intimating and explaining why pains feel inescapably "mine." Emotions similarly feature self-representational elements, where the affective tone is accompanied by an intrinsic awareness of that tone, contributing to their subjective immediacy. Phenomenal concepts—mental representations of experiential qualities—operate as self-representing in this framework, capturing the "what-it's-like" aspect through their own reflexive structure, which avoids reducing qualia to mere first-order content.[23] Variants within self-representational higher-order theories include acquaintance-based models, where the state's self-representation manifests as direct, non-conceptual acquaintance with its own content, providing a primitive form of self-awareness akin to introspective immediacy. Kriegel explores such models to account for the non-inferential nature of phenomenal self-knowledge, suggesting that the embedded higher-order element functions as an acquaintance relation rather than a full propositional thought. These variants maintain the core intrinsic approach while accommodating phenomenological intuitions about effortless self-familiarity in conscious states.

Other variants

In addition to the primary variants of higher-order theories, emerging approaches integrate statistical inference frameworks to conceptualize consciousness as arising from Bayesian higher-order predictions about first-order perceptual states. Jakob Hohwy's integration of predictive processing with higher-order thought posits that the brain generates top-down predictions at multiple hierarchical levels to minimize prediction errors, where higher-order representations evaluate the reliability of lower-level sensory inferences, thereby enabling conscious experience when uncertainties or mismatches are flagged. This view treats consciousness not as a static monitoring process but as a dynamic inference mechanism, with meta-confidence computed over first-order representations to determine phenomenal awareness. A key mechanism in this statistical approach involves the brain inferring meta-confidence in its representations through precision-weighted prediction errors; significant mismatches between predicted and actual sensory inputs at higher levels signal conscious content by amplifying error signals for further processing.[24] For instance, in Hohwy's framework, consciousness emerges when higher-order predictions about the precision of first-order states fail to align, prompting adaptive revisions that bring perceptual content into awareness. Another variant emphasizes the radical plasticity of higher-order representations, suggesting they are not innate but learned and adaptable through experience, as explored in Stephen Fleming's work on metacognitive adaptability from 2021 to 2023. This thesis argues that metacognitive processes, central to higher-order theories, develop via reinforcement learning and environmental interactions, allowing individuals to calibrate confidence judgments and adjust higher-order monitoring over time. Fleming's research demonstrates how metacognitive sensitivity can be trained, supporting the idea that conscious self-awareness arises from plastic higher-order mechanisms that evolve with expertise and feedback.[25] Post-2020 developments include hybrid models that combine higher-order thought with global broadcasting, where conscious states result from higher-order representations being amplified and disseminated across neural networks for widespread access. These hybrids reconcile higher-order monitoring with the broadcast of information in global workspace frameworks, proposing that higher-order thoughts trigger ignition events that make first-order contents globally available. Recent applications in artificial intelligence, particularly from 2024-2025, explore higher-order layers in neural networks to simulate consciousness, drawing on predictive processing to model meta-representations in large language models and neuromorphic systems. For example, 2025 research examines meta-representations as representations of processes in computational models of higher-order theories.[26]

Motivations

Philosophical motivations

Higher-order theories of consciousness (HOTs) provide a compelling philosophical framework for distinguishing between unconscious and conscious mental states by positing that a first-order state becomes conscious precisely when it is the target of a suitable higher-order representation, such as a thought or perception about that state itself.[27] This approach, often grounded in the transitivity principle—which holds that a mental state is conscious if and only if one is conscious of being in that state—explains how identical first-order contents can yield either unconscious or conscious experiences depending on the presence of higher-order awareness.[27] A key philosophical appeal of HOTs lies in their functionalist response to the hard problem of consciousness, which questions why physical processes give rise to subjective experience or "what it is like" to undergo a mental state.[28] Unlike dualistic views that posit an irreducible ontological gap between physical mechanisms and phenomenal qualities, HOTs explain the subjective character of experience through representational processes, where higher-order states confer consciousness by conceptualizing first-order qualities in a way that aligns with the subject's perspective.[27] This sidesteps Chalmers' explanatory gap by treating phenomenal "what it is likeness" as derivative of cognitive architecture rather than a primitive feature demanding non-physical explanation, thereby reducing the hard problem to tractable issues of representation and monitoring.[28][27] By embedding consciousness within a naturalistic cognitive framework, HOTs avoid the pitfalls of dualism, which requires positing separate mental substances or intrinsic properties beyond physical laws.[27] Instead, they ground phenomenal awareness in the same representational mechanisms that underpin ordinary cognition, such as higher-order thoughts that monitor and integrate first-order states without invoking extra-ontological entities.[27] This integration aligns HOTs with physicalism, explaining consciousness as an emergent feature of complex information processing in the brain's architecture.[27] HOTs also elegantly accommodate introspection and self-knowledge by viewing these as intensified forms of higher-order monitoring, where deliberate attention amplifies awareness of one's mental states to enable reliable reporting and reflection.[27] In this view, self-knowledge arises not from direct acquaintance but from higher-order representations that make internal states accessible, consistent with the reportability criterion often associated with conscious experience.[27] However, HOTs diverge from first-order representationalism, which attributes subjectivity solely to the content of world-directed representations; HOTs insist on a meta-level higher-order component to capture the for-me-ness of experience, arguing that mere first-order content lacks the self-referential structure needed for genuine phenomenal subjectivity.[27] This meta-representational requirement highlights a core debate, as representationalists contend it introduces unnecessary complexity, while HOT proponents maintain it is essential for explaining the distinctive immediacy and ownership of conscious states.[27]

Empirical motivations

Empirical phenomena such as blindsight demonstrate dissociations between low-level sensory processing and conscious awareness, motivating higher-order theories (HOTs) by suggesting that first-order representations alone are insufficient for consciousness without meta-representational monitoring.[29] In blindsight patients, who suffer damage to the primary visual cortex, individuals can accurately discriminate visual stimuli in their blind field—such as detecting motion or shape—yet report no conscious experience of seeing them, indicating intact first-order processing but absence of higher-order representation that would render the perception conscious.[29] Similarly, hemispatial neglect syndrome, often following right-hemisphere damage, involves failure to attend to stimuli in the contralesional space despite evidence of subcortical processing, as shown by reflexive orienting or priming effects; this supports HOTs by illustrating how disrupted higher-order attentional mechanisms can eliminate awareness while preserving basic sensory input.[29] Change blindness provides further empirical motivation for HOTs, revealing failures in conscious tracking of environmental changes despite ongoing first-order visual processing.[1] In classic paradigms, such as those where scenes flicker between versions differing by salient features like object color or position, participants often miss changes when a brief blank intervenes, yet behavioral measures confirm that low-level detection occurs; HOT proponents argue this arises because higher-order states misrepresent or fail to update the first-order content, leading to illusory continuity in awareness.[30] This dissociation underscores the need for meta-representational processes to bind and maintain conscious perception, as first-order theories struggle to explain why detected changes evade reportability.[1] Libet’s experiments from the 1980s highlight temporal delays in conscious awareness, aligning with HOTs' view that consciousness involves a retrospective higher-order construction rather than direct first-order immediacy.[31] In these studies, participants reported the timing of their urge to flex a finger, but readiness potentials in motor cortex preceded awareness by approximately 350 milliseconds, with subjective timing backdated; this suggests that the conscious experience is not simultaneous with the initiating neural event but emerges via higher-order monitoring that integrates and attributes the state post hoc.[31] Such findings motivate HOTs over simpler theories by implying that awareness requires a secondary representational layer to confer subjective timing and intentionality.[29] Studies on animal metacognition offer motivation for dispositional variants of HOTs, as non-human animals exhibit behaviors suggestive of uncertainty monitoring without necessitating actual higher-order thoughts.[32] For instance, in perceptual uncertainty tasks, animals like rats and pigeons opt out of difficult trials to avoid errors, showing sensitivity to confidence akin to human metacognition; dispositional HOT theory posits this as readiness for higher-order representation rather than explicit meta-thoughts, accommodating limited primate capacities while explaining adaptive decision-making.[32] This framework contrasts with stricter actualist HOTs and supports broader applicability of higher-order approaches to consciousness across species.[33] Recent adversarial collaborations from 2023 to 2025 have tested HOTs against first-order theories using masking paradigms, providing updated empirical scrutiny.[34] In structured projects funded by the Templeton Foundation, teams led by researchers like Biyu He examined neural signatures during visual masking, where stimuli near perceptual thresholds elicit unconscious processing; these efforts, including multi-site EEG and fMRI data, highlight ongoing empirical validation of higher-order meta-representation in borderline conscious states as of 2025.[34][35]

Scientific perspectives

Neuroscientific evidence

Neuroscientific investigations provide support for higher-order theories (HOTs) of consciousness by identifying neural correlates of higher-order processing, particularly in regions involved in metacognitive monitoring and integration of perceptual content. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have demonstrated that activity in the rostrolateral prefrontal cortex (rlPFC) tracks metacognitive sensitivity during perceptual decision-making, where participants report confidence in their choices, indicating a role in higher-order representation of first-order perceptual states.[36] Studies using transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) in humans further corroborate this, showing that temporary disruption of the anterior prefrontal cortex selectively impairs metacognitive accuracy in visual discrimination tasks, such as estimating perceptual confidence, while leaving basic perceptual performance intact. These findings suggest that prefrontal regions are crucial for the higher-order monitoring posited by HOTs.[37] The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) contributes to higher-order confidence signals, particularly in error detection and performance monitoring. A 2023 meta-analysis highlights consistent activation in the dorsal ACC during tasks involving decision uncertainty and error likelihood prediction, aligning with its proposed function in generating higher-order evaluations of cognitive outcomes.[38] This activity is thought to reflect metacognitive processes that assess the reliability of first-order representations, supporting HOT frameworks where consciousness arises from such reflective monitoring.[39] Connectivity analyses using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) post-2020 emphasize thalamo-cortical loops as key for integrating first-order sensory inputs with higher-order cortical processing. Studies show that structural connections between higher-order thalamic nuclei, such as the mediodorsal thalamus, and prefrontal areas enable the relay and amplification of sensory signals into metacognitive representations, with maturation along the sensorimotor-association cortical axis.[40] These loops facilitate the transformation of basic perceptual data into conscious awareness, consistent with HOTs' emphasis on hierarchical representation.[41] In non-human primates, neural correlates of consciousness (NCCs) related to higher-order processes are evident in prefrontal activation during confidence tasks. Chemical inactivation of the prefrontal cortex in monkeys impairs metacognitive judgments in memory tasks, demonstrating a causal role in evaluating decision certainty.[42] Seminal recordings by Kiani and Shadlen (2009) revealed population coding of confidence in parietal areas during motion discrimination, with extensions in 2025 linking prefrontal circuits to similar confidence propagation in hierarchical decisions.[43][44] Recent circuit-level models in 2025 further bolster HOTs by linking dendritic integration to higher-order amplification. In layer 5b pyramidal neurons, dendritic compartments integrate distal thalamic inputs with somatic signals, gating conscious content through content-matching mechanisms, as outlined in dendritic integration theory.[45] A review in Trends in Cognitive Sciences highlights how these processes, tested via laminar MEG and high-field fMRI, provide a mechanistic basis for higher-order theories, distinguishing them from lower-level sensory processing.[45]

Behavioral evidence

Behavioral evidence for higher-order theories of consciousness draws from psychological experiments demonstrating capacities for monitoring and reflecting on one's own mental states through observable actions and decisions. In humans, metacognition tasks reveal that confidence judgments about perceptual decisions correlate with the accuracy of higher-order representations of those decisions. Using signal detection theory, researchers quantify this through measures like meta-d', which assesses how well subjective confidence tracks objective performance independent of task difficulty. For instance, in visual discrimination tasks, participants' confidence ratings show higher sensitivity when higher-order monitoring is engaged, supporting the idea that conscious experience involves meta-representations of first-order states.[46][47] Comparative studies in non-human animals provide evidence of uncertainty monitoring without reliance on language, aligning with dispositional higher-order thought theories that posit implicit meta-awareness. A 2023 meta-analysis of opt-out and information-seeking tasks found that rats and pigeons reliably choose to avoid uncertain trials or seek clarifying information, with pooled effect sizes indicating adaptive metacognitive-like behavior (e.g., composite score effect size of 0.173, 95% CI [0.112, 0.233]). Rats exhibited an 81.5% success rate in information-seeking paradigms, while pigeons succeeded in 63.6% of cases, suggesting these species access dispositional higher-order representations of their cognitive states to guide behavior.[48] Illusion susceptibility further illustrates the role of higher-order endorsement in establishing body ownership. In the rubber hand illusion paradigm, synchronous visuotactile stimulation leads to perceived ownership of a fake limb only when participants actively endorse the multisensory congruence at a higher-order level, as measured by proprioceptive drift and explicit ownership ratings. Related virtual hand illusion studies show that inducing metacognitive divergent thinking enhances susceptibility to ownership feelings, indicating that reflective monitoring modulates the integration of bodily representations.[49][50] Developmental research tracks the emergence of higher-order processes through longitudinal observations of infant behaviors. Post-2020 studies demonstrate that gaze following, where infants shift attention to objects based on an adult's direction, predicts later metacognitive abilities, such as mental-state language production by age 2.5 years, with higher early gaze-following scores correlating with advanced theory-of-mind development. This suggests gaze following reflects nascent higher-order representations of others' attentional states, foundational to self-monitoring.[51] Recent virtual reality experiments from 2024-2025 explore higher-order monitoring under altered perceptual states induced by simulated psychedelics. In VR setups mimicking hallucinogenic visuals, participants report reduced metacognitive insight into perceptual accuracy, mirroring pharmacological psychedelic effects where confidence judgments decouple from performance. These findings indicate that disrupting higher-order perceptual representations impairs uncertainty monitoring, providing behavioral support for theories emphasizing meta-representational fragility in non-ordinary states.[52]

Criticisms

Objections to higher-order thought theories

One prominent objection to higher-order thought (HOT) theories, both actualist and dispositional variants, is the "animal and infant problem," which contends that these theories implausibly deny phenomenal consciousness to non-human animals and human infants lacking sophisticated metacognitive abilities. According to HOT accounts, a first-order mental state becomes conscious only through accompaniment by a higher-order thought representing it, a capacity typically attributed to beings with advanced conceptual resources for self-monitoring. However, empirical evidence suggests that many animals and preverbal infants exhibit behaviors indicative of phenomenal experience, such as pain responses, without evidence of such metacognition; for instance, studies on neonatal pain reactions and animal avoidance learning imply subjective suffering that HOTs cannot accommodate without ad hoc adjustments.[53] This critique, originally articulated by Ned Block, highlights a tension between HOTs and the broad distribution of consciousness suggested by evolutionary and developmental psychology.[54] Ongoing debates as of 2025 continue to challenge HOTs on this front, with proponents of animal consciousness arguing that behavioral indicators like empathy in primates and distress in cephalopods—supported by recent 2024–2025 neuroscientific reviews—undermine the metacognitive requirement, favoring first-order theories instead.[55] Another key criticism is the charge of circularity, which posits that HOT theories presuppose consciousness in the higher-order thoughts they invoke to explain it. Alex Byrne argues that for a higher-order thought to render a first-order state conscious, the higher-order thought itself must already be conscious or involve conscious elements, leading to an explanatory loop where consciousness is defined in terms of itself.[56] This objection applies to both actualist HOTs, where the higher-order thought must be occurrent and conscious, and dispositional variants, which rely on the disposition to form such thoughts but still beg the question of what makes those dispositions conscious-enabling.[57] Critics maintain that this circularity undermines the theory's reductive ambitions, as it fails to ground consciousness in non-conscious processes without invoking further higher-order representations ad infinitum.[58] HOT theories also face accusations of over-intellectualization, suggesting they erroneously require conceptual or propositional thought for even the simplest conscious sensations. Alvin Goldman contends that basic perceptual experiences, such as feeling a rock's texture, possess phenomenal character without necessitating higher-order conceptual monitoring, rendering HOTs overly demanding for everyday consciousness.[59] This critique targets the theory's reliance on thoughts involving concepts like "sensation" or "experience," which infants and animals may lack, yet whose states appear consciously felt based on neurophysiological correlates like thalamic activation.[60] Proponents of HOTs respond by broadening concepts to non-linguistic forms, but detractors argue this dilutes the theory, making it indistinguishable from weaker representational accounts. The problem of targetless higher-order thoughts further challenges HOTs by highlighting cases where a higher-order thought lacks a corresponding first-order target, leading to implausible phenomenological implications. In scenarios like confabulation or certain hallucinations, an individual might form a HOT about a non-existent sensory state, yet HOT theory predicts a corresponding conscious experience despite the absence of any lower-order content, mismatching the subjective emptiness reported in such cases.[61] This objection, directed particularly at David Rosenthal's actualist HOT framework, suggests that consciousness cannot be fully explained by HOTs alone, as they generate "empty" experiences without sensory grounding, diverging from the theory's aim to account for qualia.[62] Dispositional HOTs fare no better, as the mere potential for such targetless thoughts could attribute consciousness to unrealized states, complicating empirical verification.[63] Recent critiques from artificial intelligence research, particularly as of 2023–2025, extend these concerns by examining large language models (LLMs), which feature layered architectures mimicking higher-order representations but are widely regarded as lacking consciousness. LLMs process inputs through successive "higher-order" layers that meta-represent prior activations, analogous to HOT mechanisms, yet they exhibit no subjective experience, suggesting that structural similarity to HOTs is insufficient for consciousness and exposing the theory's potential over-reliance on representational complexity. Empirical assessments of LLMs on consciousness indicators, such as self-awareness tasks, reinforce this, showing advanced metacognition without phenomenal qualities, thus challenging HOTs' applicability beyond biological systems. These AI-based objections underscore ongoing debates about whether HOTs are biologically parochial, failing to distinguish conscious from non-conscious higher-order processing in computational substrates.

Objections to higher-order perception theories

One prominent objection to higher-order perception (HOP) theories of consciousness is the problem of infinite regress, which arises because the perceptual monitoring of a first-order mental state itself requires a further higher-order perception to become conscious, leading to an unending chain of perceptions without a base case.[17] This regress threatens to undermine the theory's explanatory power, as it implies that no mental state could ever achieve consciousness without an infinite hierarchy of perceptual states, a scenario deemed implausible given finite cognitive resources.[64] A related structural critique is the modularity problem, which highlights the absence of any dedicated neural organ or mechanism for an "inner sense" analogous to the specialized organs for external perception, such as the eyes or ears.[65] Unlike first-order perceptual systems, which are modular and informationally encapsulated, no empirical evidence supports a distinct, localized "scanning" module for monitoring inner states, raising doubts about the perceptual model's biological plausibility. Critics argue that this lack of modularity suggests HOP theories borrow unexamined assumptions from outer perception without corresponding neuroanatomical support.[66] HOP theories also face a phenomenological challenge known as the qualia mismatch, where the perceptual analogy fails to account for the subjective character of non-sensory conscious states, such as thoughts or emotions, which lack the sensory-like qualities posited by the model.[67] Joseph Levine has argued that higher-order perceptual representations cannot adequately capture the intrinsic "what it's like" aspect of qualia in abstract mental states, as perception inherently involves sensory modalities that do not apply uniformly to all forms of consciousness. This mismatch implies that HOP approaches overemphasize sensory phenomenology at the expense of broader conscious experiences.[68] Empirically, HOP theories suffer from a significant gap in supporting evidence, with recent reviews indicating no clear neural or behavioral data for a perceptual-like scanning process that monitors all mental states to confer consciousness.[69] Studies on prefrontal lesions and perceptual tasks, for instance, fail to demonstrate the predicted disruption of higher-order perceptual mechanisms without affecting first-order processing, suggesting the theory's core predictions remain unverified.[70] This evidential shortfall contrasts with stronger support for alternative theories like global workspace models.[71] More recent developments in neural circuit theories emphasize dynamic interactions across distributed brain networks. For example, a 2025 review of circuit-level mechanisms, such as apical amplification and dendritic integration, explores how consciousness may emerge from integrated thalamo-cortical loops involving layer 5b pyramidal neurons, potentially providing mechanistic insights into higher-order perceptual processes.[72]

Responses and ongoing debates

Proponents of higher-order theories (HOTs) have addressed the "animal problem"—the concern that HOTs exclude non-human animals from consciousness by requiring sophisticated metacognition—through dispositional variants of the theory. In dispositional HOTs, consciousness arises from a disposition to form higher-order thoughts about first-order states, rather than requiring actual higher-order thoughts at every moment, thereby accommodating simpler creatures like animals that possess the relevant cognitive capacities without explicit metacognition. Actualist versions of HOTs refine this further by positing "weak" metacognition, where higher-order representations need only monitor reliability or confidence in first-order states, allowing for consciousness in animals with rudimentary self-monitoring abilities.[17] The regress objection, which posits an infinite chain of higher-order representations, has been countered by HOT advocates through self-representational models and acceptance of finite regresses. Self-representational HOTs embed higher-order awareness within the first-order state itself, avoiding an infinite regress by treating consciousness as an intrinsic, non-relational feature of the representation without requiring separate higher-order states.[73] Rosenthal argues that even if a limited regress occurs, it is not vicious, as higher-order states need not themselves be conscious, terminating naturally in unconscious monitoring processes that suffice for phenomenal experience.[73] Recent empirical defenses of HOTs draw on adversarial collaborations testing predictions in perceptual paradigms, including masking tasks. The HOTTEST project (2023–2026), funded by the Templeton World Charity Foundation, aims to contrast HOT predictions—that consciousness requires higher-order monitoring, leading to differential neural signatures in masking scenarios like continuous flash suppression—with first-order theories. A 2025 review in Frontiers in Science highlights masking tests and related studies as providing empirical support for HOTs, showing that disrupted higher-order access correlates with loss of reportable awareness, aligning with neural data from electrocorticography.[55][74] Ongoing debates center on integrating HOTs with other frameworks like integrated information theory (IIT) and global neuronal workspace theory (GWT), with the 2025 Cogitate consortium's adversarial testing yielding mixed results that support aspects of GWT (e.g., prefrontal decoding) and IIT (e.g., posterior activity), informing comparisons and potential hybrid models with HOTs.[75] Hybrid models propose combining HOTs' emphasis on metacognitive representation with IIT's information integration or GWT's broadcasting mechanisms to explain graded consciousness, though critics argue HOTs better account for subjective access without IIT's panpsychist implications.[76] For AI consciousness, 2025 discussions on PhilArchive explore HOT implications, suggesting that systems with recursive self-modeling could achieve consciousness, but current large language models lack genuine higher-order monitoring, raising ethical questions about future implementations.[77] Future directions include empirical testing of HOTs in altered states like anesthesia and coma, where disruptions in higher-order processing predict loss of consciousness. Studies using EEG during propofol-induced anesthesia show reduced higher-order error signals in predictive coding frameworks, supporting HOT integration with hierarchical models where top-down predictions fail without metacognitive oversight.[78] In coma research, HOTs predict recovery correlates with restored higher-order thalamocortical loops, guiding interventions via neuroimaging.[79]
